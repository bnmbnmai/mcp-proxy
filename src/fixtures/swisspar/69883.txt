3            Medical context

Hereditary angioedema (HAE) is a rare autosomal dominant disorder characterised by deficiency of C1
inhibitor; the C1 inhibitor may be truly deficient due to genetic mutations (type I HAE) or dysfunctional
(type II HAE). More than 150 mutations have been identified. The disorder is characterised by disabling
recurrent episodes of local skin swellings, painful abdominal attacks, and, occasionally, laryngeal
attacks that can be life-threatening.
Estimates of HAE prevalence range from 1 in 150,000 to 1 in 10,000, but most studies report prevalence
as 1 in 50,000-60,000. Roche et al. (2005) assessed the prevalence of HAE in Spain in July 1999 and
reported 1.09 cases per 100,000 population. The mean age of onset of symptoms was 12.6 years with
a mean age of diagnosis of 24.9 years, suggesting a significant delay in the diagnosis of HAE. Another
study in Norway reported the prevalence of HAE to be 1.75 cases per 100,000 population.
Hereditary angioedema typically first appears during childhood or adolescence and then persists
throughout life. A study by Bork et al. (2006) reported a mean age of onset of 11.2 years, and 64/209
(30%) patients with HAE had disease onset by age 5.
Patients with HAE are generally healthy apart from their HAE. However, HAE may be associated with
autoimmune diseases. In particular, systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) has been reported in
association with HAE. In one study of 157 patients with HAE, 19 (12%) had an autoimmune disorder
such as inflammatory bowel disease, Sjogren’s syndrome, lupus, or glomerulonephritis.
Treatment for HAE has two aspects. Some interventions seek to terminate acute attacks; others involve
drugs whose goal is prophylaxis against future attacks. In the past, antihistamines, corticosteroids and
epinephrine were utilised for acute attacks, despite a lack of evidence that they were effective, but there
were no HAE-specific treatments then. Since 2008, various forms of C1 inhibitor have become
available. Two randomised placebo-controlled trials demonstrated the efficacy of Cinryze, a C1 inhibitor
(human) concentrate which is now approved in Europe. Other similar agents, including ecallantide and
icatibant, are FDA-approved and available in the US for acute attacks. Icatibant is also approved in the
EU and in Switzerland.
For long-term prophylaxis, androgens have been utilised in the past under the rationale that they
stimulate the production of C1 inhibitor. However, although they are effective in the prevention of
attacks, it is generally believed that their toxicity limits their utility for long-term use. Antifibrinolytics,
such as tranexamic acid, have also been utilised for this purpose, but careful studies have not clearly
demonstrated efficacy. Lanadelumab and berotralstat, kallikrein inhibitors, are available for use as
prophylactic agents.

5            Nonclinical aspects

5.1          Pharmacology

Primary Pharmacodynamics
Hereditary angioedema (HAE) is a rare and potentially life-threatening autosomal dominant inherited
disease leading to inadvertent vascular permeability. It is often caused by mutations in the
complement 1 inhibitor gene (C1-INH), resulting in uncontrolled activation of plasma prekallikrein
(PKK) and subsequent formation of bradykinin.

Donidalorsen (ISIS 721744), is a GalNAc3-linker conjugated chemically modified (2’-MOE, PS,
among others) 20-nucleotide antisense oligonucleotide (ASO), specifically designed to degrade PKK
mRNA via RNase H1 activation. Donidalorsen and several variants were tested in vitro in cultured
hepatocellular carcinoma cells and primary human hepatocytes, and in vivo in mice and monkeys.
Donidalorsen is not pharmacologically active in the mouse due to sequence differences between the
human and the mouse PKK mRNA. The in vivo pharmacodynamics of donidalorsen was investigated
in transgenic mice expressing the human PKK gene or in wild-type C57BL/6J mice with an
unconjugated mouse surrogate ASO. Donidalorsen was shown to efficiently downregulate PKK
mRNA in human cells and, after subcutaneous (SC) injection, in human-PKK transgenic mice and
monkeys. The GalNAc3 modification increased the potency of uptake of the ASO by hepatocytes by
46-fold in cells in culture and by about 10-fold in mice. In monkeys, donidalorsen doses of 6, 12 and
30 mg/kg/wk resulted in 58%, 60% and 62% reductions, respectively, in hepatic PKK mRNA levels.
PKK plasma protein activity was also dose-dependently reduced by 75%, 79% and 87% at 2, 6, and
12 mg/kg/wk, respectively. Both PKK liver mRNA expression and plasma protein activity levels were
completely restored after a 26-week recovery period. In mice, it was shown that experimentally
induced vascular permeability was decreased by mouse-PKK-specific surrogate ASOs. The provided
off-target analyses and proof-of-concept can be accepted.

Safety Pharmacology
In vitro, the hERG assay was used to assess the potential of donidalorsen to induce prolongation of
the QT interval. Donidalorsen did not exhibit statistically significant hERG inhibition when compared to
the vehicle at concentrations up to 300 µM.

In vivo, a single administration of donidalorsen was used to evaluate potential effects on
cardiovascular, respiratory, and central nervous system (CNS) function in monkeys. No critical effects
on cardiovascular, respiratory or neurobehavioural parameters were identified after SC administration
of donidalorsen at 12 or 30 mg/kg.

Pharmacodynamic Drug Interactions
No dedicated pharmacodynamic studies have been performed. However, donidalorsen interference
with warfarin and ibuprofen plasma protein binding was investigated. Donidalorsen is highly bound (≥
96%) to plasma proteins. The protein binding affinity is relatively weak. No drug-drug interaction with
regards to plasma protein binding between donidalorsen and warfarin and ibuprofen were noted.


5.2          Pharmacokinetics

Absorption
Donidalorsen was rapidly absorbed in mice and monkeys after SC administration, with a Tmax of 1–2
hours. Plasma donidalorsen concentrations declined in a multiphasic manner with a rapid distribution
phase, followed by a slower elimination phase with mean half-life values ranging from approximately 2
to 5 weeks in monkeys. Plasma PK was unaltered after repeated dosing for up to 39 weeks in
monkeys. Plasma trough concentrations were shown to be in equilibrium with tissues.

Distribution
In mice, liver and kidney are the main organs of donidalorsen accumulation. Liver and kidney
concentrations increased with increasing dose. There was no tendency to accumulate in tissues over
time. After a 13-week recovery period, donidalorsen decreased below the limit of quantitation. In
studies with respect to reproductive toxicity, donidalorsen was below the limit of quantitation in
placenta samples, foetal liver samples and in breast milk from lactating parental females.
In rats, radioactively labelled donidalorsen distributed to many tissues. Radioactivity in the kidney was
primarily associated with the cortex. There was no notable gender difference. At the 2 mg/kg dose
level, Tmax in the liver was 24 hours and in the kidney 158 hours. At the 10 mg/kg dose level, Tmax was
8 hours in the liver and around 48 hours for at least half of the analysed tissues.
In monkeys, data from toxicokinetic evaluations showed that donidalorsen distributed extensively to
kidney cortex and liver. Unconjugated donidalorsen concentrations were dose-proportional in kidney
cortex, while less than dose-proportional in liver, suggesting saturation of liver uptake at higher dose
levels. Tissue concentrations decreased in a monophasic pattern. The half-life for a 2 mg/kg dose
level in the kidney cortex was 18.6 days and in the liver 12.1 days, and was generally comparable to
the estimated plasma elimination half-life (14.1 days).

Metabolism
Unconjugated donidalorsen was the major species of full-length oligonucleotide present in kidney and
liver samples at the analysed time points. In mice, unconjugated donidalorsen accounted for
approximately 93.1% and 96.4% of the total oligonucleotides present on Day 93. Numerous shortmer
oligonucleotide metabolites of unconjugated donidalorsen were evident in the examined samples. The
overall metabolic pathways for the oligonucleotide-related metabolites of donidalorsen observed
across species is consistent with the endo- and exonuclease-mediated hydrolysis of the
deoxyphosphorothioate linkages to form chain-shortened metabolites in tissues which are rapidly
excreted in urine. The THA-linker-related metabolism is generally similar across species, with the
formation of oxidative metabolites that are eliminated through urine and bile.

Excretion
In mice, donidalorsen and its unconjugated full-length oligonucleotide metabolite were the major
components in urine samples. Metabolites generated by endonuclease and exonuclease cleavage
were also identified.
In rats, excretion of radioactively labelled donidalorsen occurred via the faecal and urinary routes.
Within 48 hours, around 14% of the dose was excreted, with a mean of 1.5% of the dose recovered in
urine, and around 12% in faeces. Overall, around 58% of the dose was excreted in faeces and 17% in
urine.
In urine samples of monkeys, donidalorsen and unconjugated donidalorsen were the major
components. Other metabolites detected in urine from monkeys were 3′-deletion chain-shortened
metabolites of donidalorsen with -2 and -3 GalNAc sugar deletions, as well as metabolites of
unconjugated donidalorsen with loss of nucleotides from both the 3′- and 5′-ends.

The analysis of the excretion of the THA-linker and its metabolites identified 10 metabolites in mice
and monkeys in urine and faeces. The concentrations of the quantifiable metabolites M8 and M12
were substantially higher in monkey faeces than in both plasma (> 1000x) and urine (> 5x). Therefore,
faeces is the major route of elimination for the linker-related metabolites.

Pharmacokinetic Drug Interactions
Treatment of cryopreserved human primary hepatocytes with donidalorsen did not cause inhibition of
CYP1A2, CYP2B6, CYP2C8, CYP2C9, CYP2C19, CYP2D6, CYP2E1, or CYP3A4 enzyme activity.
With respect to transporters, donidalorsen (at 100 µM) did not inhibit more than 50% of human OAT1,
OAT3, OCT1, OCT2, OATP1B1, OATP1B3, MATE1, MATE2-K, BCRP, P-gp, and BSEP transporters,
and no effects were noted at lower concentrations.

The M8 linker metabolite was found to inhibit CYP2B6, CYP2C9, CYP2C19 and CYP3A4 at 100 μM
in human liver microsomes. CYP3A4 may be able to metabolise the M8 compound, the consumption
was slow and produced metabolites that could only be detected at high incubation concentrations.
The M8 metabolite was also tested for interaction / interference with human transporters. M8 inhibited

5.3          Toxicology

The nonclinical toxicology studies included 13- and 26-week repeat dose studies in the CD-1 mouse,
and 13- and 39-week studies in the cynomolgus monkey. A battery of genotoxicity testing, a 27-week
carcinogenicity study in Tg.rasH2 mice and a 2-year carcinogenicity study in rats were performed. In
addition, a combined fertility and early embryonic development / embryo-foetal development study
and a pre- and postnatal study were performed in mice. A 13-week impurity qualification study was
performed in mice. A repeat-dose toxicity study in juvenile mice was performed to support a potential
expansion of the indication to paediatric patients between 2 and < 12 years of age.

The main findings in mice at high dose levels were increased liver weights with signs of liver toxicity
(increased ALT, AST, ALP blood levels, hepatocyte hypertrophy, necrosis, increased mitotic rates),
increased spleen weights and inguinal lymph node enlargements. Liver hepatocytes, Kupffer cells and
macrophages displayed vacuolated / granular staining due to the extensive uptake of donidalorsen.
Accumulation of vacuolated / granular macrophages was observed in several tissues, including
injection sites. Effects in kidneys were limited to the accumulation of basophilic granules in proximal
tubular epithelial cells. These effects were reversible or showed signs of reversibility. No toxicological
finding related to PKK mRNA knockdown was observed. The NOAEL in mice was defined as 12
mg/kg/q2w.

The main findings in monkeys at 12 and 30 mg/kg/week donidalorsen dose levels were severe, but
manageable, PLT reduction events in two animals, reversible increases in CRP, transient
prolongation of aPTT and elevated levels of the complement factor Bb. Additional findings were
injection site reactions (ISRs) (all groups), increased liver weight with associated hepatocyte
hypertrophy, basophilic granules in hepatocytes and Kupffer cells), increased kidney weights with
accumulation of basophilic granules within tubular epithelial cells, reactions in mesenteric /
mandibular lymph nodes, and perivascular/vascular inflammations in several tissues. The findings
reversed or showed signs of reversibility. No toxicological finding related to PKK mRNA knockdown
was noted. In the keyhole limpet haemocyanin (KLH) antigenic challenge, donidalorsen was
associated with decreases in mean anti-KLH IgM at 6 and 12 mg/kg/week. Anti-donidalorsen
antibodies were noted in the toxicity studies, although these did not seem to have any effects on
tissue exposure levels. The NOAEL in monkeys was considered as 6 mg/kg/week.

No critical findings were noted in the assessments regarding genotoxicity (Ames test, chromosome
aberration assay, in vivo mammalian micronucleus assay), reproductive and development toxicity in
mice, pre- and postnatal development in mice, SC treatment of juvenile mice with donidalorsen and
impurity qualification.

No carcinoma development was observed in a 6-month carcinogenicity study in Tg.ras2 transgenic
mice or in a 2-year carcinogenicity study in rats. Hepatocellular adenomas were present in two out of
25 males at 20 mg/kg/q2w. In the 26-week repeat dose study in CD-1 mice, liver adenoma
development was also observed in two male animals (at 1 and 12 mg/kg dose levels). In both studies
the incidence of hepatocellular adenomas in donidalorsen-treated animals was close to historical

control data and did not show dose-dependence. Of note, mice are more sensitive to liver reactions
than other species after repeated treatment with GalNAc3-linker conjugated ASOs.


5.4          Nonclinical conclusions

The nonclinical data showed that SC administered GalNAc3-linker conjugated donidalorsen was
selectively taken up by hepatocytes and efficiently downregulated PKK mRNA, resulting in decreased
vascular permeability. Safety pharmacology assessments did not reveal critical cardiovascular,
respiratory, or CNS effects. No drug-drug interaction with regards to plasma protein binding between
donidalorsen and other highly plasma protein bound drugs are expected.

The pharmacokinetic assessment of donidalorsen showed the expected profile of GalNAc3-linker
conjugated chemically modified ASOs with rapid absorption after SC administration, rapid distribution
to many tissues and an elimination phase of 2-5 weeks. As expected, the main tissues of
donidalorsen accumulation are the liver and the kidney. No measurable donidalorsen concentrations
were noted in placenta, breast milk and foetal liver samples, suggesting no distribution to offspring.
The metabolic pathway of the oligonucleotide part is consistent with the endo- and exonuclease-
mediated hydrolysis and excretion via the faecal and urinary routes. Several THA-linker metabolites
were identified in urine and faeces. Faeces is the major route of elimination for the linker-related
metabolites. Pharmacokinetic drug interaction studies did not show critical effects on a battery of CYP
enzymes and human transporters within a safety margin of around 8-fold as compared to the clinical
relevant exposure.

The nonclinical toxicological profile of repeat dose treatment with donidalorsen showed an acceptable
safety profile. During clinical treatment, PLT events, liver and kidney parameters need to be
monitored. The identified NOAELs allow safety margins of up to 29-fold in monkeys (cumulative
exposures) and up to 5-fold in mice (cumulative exposures) with respect to the clinical dose of 80 mg
donidalorsen. These are acceptable ranges, although the safety margin as calculated in mice is
relatively low.

No carcinoma development was observed in a 6-month carcinogenicity study in Tg.ras2 transgenic
mice and in a 2-year carcinogenicity study in rats. Hepatocellular adenomas were present in a few
male mice. The incidence rates are close to historical control data and do not show dose-
dependence. In addition, it can be accepted that mice are known to be sensitive to liver reactions after
repeated treatment with GalNAc3-linker conjugated ASOs. Therefore, the overall weight of evidence,
including low and variable incidence, lack of dose-response, benign histopathology, alignment with
historical control data, no neoplastic findings in the 2-year rat study, and lack of translation to non-
rodent species, the observed liver adenomas in mice are not considered indicative of a human
carcinogenic risk.

No critical findings were noted in the assessments regarding genotoxicity (Ames test, chromosome
aberration assay, in vivo mammalian micronucleus assay), reproductive and development toxicity in
mice, pre- and postnatal development in mice, SC treatment of juvenile mice with donidalorsen and
impurity qualification.

Overall, in can be concluded that the nonclinical assessment of donidalorsen is complete and the
benefit outweighs the risk. From the nonclinical perspective, donidalorsen can be approved for the
treatment of HAE.

6            Clinical aspects

6.1          Clinical pharmacology

Bioanalytics
Donidalorsen was quantified in human plasma with a validated hybridisation ECL method. The
method detects the total full-length ASO, independently of the constitution of the GalNAc conjugate.
Around Cmax at steady state, the relative abundance of intact donidalorsen was found to be 94.5% of
the total full-length ASO, whereas the full-length ASO with one, two or three GalNAc sugar moiety
deletions, corresponding to 3.7%, 1.2% and 0.6%, respectively, of the relative abundance. Thus, the
donidalorsen plasma concentration measured by the ECL method predominantly consists of the
conjugated ASO species.

Biopharmaceutical development
The clinical formulation used during clinical development was a 100 mg/mL donidalorsen solution
filled into vials and administered subcutaneously by a disposable dosing syringe. The commercial
formulation is an autoinjector comprised of a glass prefilled syringe (PFS) assembled into a single-use
autoinjector. Bioequivalence (BE) between the two formulations was demonstrated.

Pharmacokinetics
The pharmacokinetics of donidalorsen was analysed using a PopPK model with healthy volunteer and
patient data. The final model was a two-compartment model with a biphasic elimination rate.

Absorption
After SC administration, donidalorsen was rapidly absorbed into the systemic circulation, with a Tmax
at approximately 1-3 hours post dose. The accumulation of donidalorsen is negligible.

Distribution
Donidalorsen is highly bound to plasma proteins, independently of the concentrations tested. Approx
98% of donidalorsen was bound to human plasma proteins over the range of concentrations tested.
The distribution of donidalorsen was assessed in vivo in the non-clinical evaluation. After SC
administration, donidalorsen is predominantly distributed to liver and kidney.

Metabolism
The GalNAc-conjugated ASO consists of two different parts undergoing different metabolic pathways.
The ASO moiety is cleaved by exo- and endonucleases, whereas the GalNAc linker is cleaved by
hydrolysis and is further oxidated.

The unconjugated full-length ASO is the active principle of the molecule. After endocytosis into
hepatocytes, a fast hydrolysis of the linker by glycosidases can be assumed. This is supported by
non-clinical data from toxicology studies in cynomolgus monkeys, where predominantly the
unconjugated ASO was found in liver tissue. The unconjugated ASO undergoes metabolism by
endonucleases and exonucleases, leading to shortmers. The shortmers are then further eliminated
through renal clearance. The metabolites were analysed in mouse, monkey and human urine, and a
similar biotransformation was found across species. The predominant human metabolites in urine
were the unconjugated full-length ASO (20-mer), accounting for approx. 40 % of the relative
metabolites found in urine, and 5’-shortmers (9 to14-mers) thereof, accounting for less than 10%
each. In plasma, no shortmers could be identified.

The metabolic pathway of the linker was assessed in mice, monkey and human plasma samples by
MS/MS. M8 and M12 were the predominant linker metabolites found in monkey and human plasma
samples, corresponding to approx. 1.5% of the parent concentration.

Elimination
Donidalorsen shows a biphasic elimination phase in plasma. The first elimination phase is attributed
to a fast distribution of the donidalorsen into tissues (predominantly into liver and kidney). After 24
hours the majority of donidalorsen is no longer available in plasma.
The population estimate of the terminal elimination half-life of donidalorsen in a typical HAE patient is
31.4 days (approximately 1 month). This parameter is of low clinical relevance, since the terminal half-
life likely represents the unconjugated full-length ASO. The slowly formed fragmented oligonucleotide
metabolites were subsequently excreted in urine. A strong biliary excretion of linker derived
metabolites is seen in nonclinical analysis.

Special populations / Intrinsic factors

Body weight
The body weight (BW) has an effect on the exposure of donidalorsen, with higher BW leading to lower
exposure The BW effect was estimated in the PopPK model and was included as a factor on Cl/f,
Vc/F, Q/F, and Vp/F. A similar response rate in efficacy can be expected across the investigated BW
range.
Due to the BW-dependent exposure, it can be expected that plasma exposure levels above the
studied range are reached in patients below 35 kg.

Age and gender
No gender- or age-dependent effects in addition to the BW effect were observed.

Hepatic impairment
Two patients with mild hepatic impairment showed no differences in exposure. No dose adjustment
for mild hepatic impairment is warranted. Moderate and severe hepatic impairment was not
investigated. Therefore, the exposure and exposure-response relationship is unknown due to the
unknown ASGPR expression status required for liver uptake.

Renal impairment
The predominant elimination pathway of the unconjugated full-length ASO (20-mer) and shortmers
thereof is renal, and no data are available for patients with moderate to severe renal impairment.


6.2          Dose finding and dose recommendation

Dose finding was limited to 80 mg either 4 weekly or 8 weekly. Donidalorsen administered every 4 or
8 weeks produced statistically significant reductions in the HAE attack rate (number of investigator-
confirmed HAE attacks per 4 weeks) compared to placebo in patients weighing ≥ 35 kg.
The reduction in dosing frequency from Q1M to Q2M for patients achieving a strong reduction in the
HAE attack rate is deemed adequate based on the exposure-response relationship of donidalorsen.

6.3          Efficacy

The efficacy data are derived from one Phase 3 pivotal study (CS5), one Phase 2a study (CS2), one
open label extension study (CS3), one OLE/Switch study (CS7) and two Phase 1 studies.
The main study (CS5) involved 91 patients from 12 years of age with hereditary angioedema who
received donidalorsen (either every 4 weeks or every 8 weeks) or placebo. After 24 weeks, the average
number of attacks per month was around 0.4 in patients given donidalorsen every 4 weeks and around
1 for those given donidalorsen every 8 weeks compared with around 2.3 for those given placebo. In
addition, the study assessed the impact of treatment on patients’ quality of life using a validated
questionnaire measuring how the disease symptoms affect people’s daily life.
Overall, the results of pivotal Study ISIS 721744-CS5 show that donidalorsen 80 mg administered
subcutaneously every 4 weeks or every 8 weeks as a prophylactic treatment resulted in clinically

The safety data are derived from one Phase 3 pivotal study (CS5), one Phase 2a study (CS2), one

Mechanism of action

Donidalorsen is a 2’-O-methoxyethyl–modified antisense oligonucleotide (ASO) conjugated to a
triantennary N-acetylgalactosamine (GalNAc3) moiety that causes ribonuclease H1 (RNase H1)
mediated degradation of prekallikrein (PKK) mRNA through selective binding to PKK mRNA, which
results in reduced production of PKK protein. PKK is a pro-enzyme for plasma kallikrein, which results
in the release of bradykinin, a potent vasodilator causing swelling and pain in HAE. In patients with
HAE, C1 inhibitor (C1-INH) deficiency or dysfunction leads to excessive plasma kallikrein activity,
bradykinin generation, and angioedema attacks. Donidalorsen lowers PKK concentration, preventing
excessive bradykinin production in patients with HAE.

Pharmacodynamics

In Trial 1 in adult and paediatric patients (≥12 years) with HAE 1 or HAE 2 (see clinical efficacy
below), a decrease in plasma PKK concentrations was observed at the first assessment (Week 4)
following treatment with Dawnzera 80 mg. The mean percentage change from baseline to Week 24 in
trough plasma PKK concentrations indicated reductions of 73% and 47% following treatment with
Dawnzera 80 mg every 4 weeks and every 8 weeks, respectively, compared with a slight increase
(2%) observed in the placebo group.

multicenter, randomised, double blind, placebo-controlled trial (Trial 3).

Trial 3 – Phase 2 Trial, ISIS 721744-CS2
Trial 3 included 20 adult patients (≥18 years) with at least 2 investigator-confirmed attacks during the
8-week run-in period, who received at least 1 dose of study treatment. Patients were randomised in a
2:1 ratio to Dawnzera 80 mg once every 4 weeks or placebo once every 4 weeks. Patients were
required to discontinue other prophylactic HAE medications prior to entering the trial; however, all
patients were allowed to use rescue medications for treatment of any breakthrough HAE attacks.
Overall, 90% of patients had HAE 1. A history of laryngeal angioedema attacks was reported in 60%
of patients and no patients were on prior prophylactic therapy. The mean attack rate during the
prospective run-in period (baseline attack rate) was 2.7 attacks/4 weeks and an attack rate of ≥2
attacks/4 weeks were observed in 65% of patients overall.
Dawnzera produced a clinically significant reduction in the HAE attack rate (number of investigator-
confirmed HAE attacks per 4 weeks) compared to placebo, meeting the primary endpoint in this trial,
as shown in Figure 2 and Table 5.

No clinically significant prolongation of QTc interval was observed at the recommended dosage of

Immunogenicity
The observed incidence of anti-drug antibodies (ADAs) is highly dependent on the sensitivity and
specificity of the assay. Differences in assay methods preclude meaningful comparisons of the
incidence of ADAs in the studies described below with the incidence of ADAs in other studies,
including those of donidalorsen or of other ASO products.
In Trial 1, with a treatment duration up to 24 weeks, the incidence rate of treatment emergent ADAs in
adult and paediatric patients (≥12 years) with HAE was 20% (9 of 45 patients) in the Dawnzera 80 mg
q4wks group and 22% (5 of 23 patients) in the Dawnzera 80 mg q8wks group. In Trial 2, an open
label extension trial, patients that rolled over from Trial 1 continued treatment with Dawnzera in the 80
mg q4wks or q8wks groups for up to 3 years (current median exposure duration of 227 days) and
showed an incidence rate of treatment emergent ADAs of 36% (25 of 69 patients) in the Dawnzera 80
mg q4wks group and 21% (3 of 14 patients) in the Dawnzera 80 mg q8wks group. The incidence rate
of treatment emergent ADAs was 42% (27 of 64 patients) for the non-rollover, Dawnzera naïve
patients who were previously treated with berotralstat, C1 esterase inhibitors, or lanadelumab before

Elderly patients
The safety and effectiveness of Dawnzera for prophylaxis to prevent attacks of HAE were evaluated
in a subgroup of patients 65 years and older. Use of Dawnzera in this population is supported by
evidence from Trials 1 and 3 that included 3 geriatric patients (≥65 years) supplemented by 2
Dawnzera naïve geriatric patients from an open label trial (Trial 2) that were previously maintained on
other HAE prophylactic therapies and not previously enrolled in Trial 1. The safety profile and
reduction in HAE attack rate in these geriatric patients were similar to those observed in younger adult
patients.


Paediatric population
The safety and effectiveness of Dawnzera for prevention of attacks of HAE have been studied in
paediatric patients 12 years and older. Use of Dawnzera in this population is supported by evidence
from Trial 1 that included adults and a total of 7 adolescent patients (12 to 17 years) supplemented by
4 Dawnzera naïve adolescent patients from an open label trial (Trial 2) that were previously
maintained on other HAE prophylactic therapies and not previously enrolled in Trial 1. The safety
profile and reduction in HAE attack rate in these adolescents were similar to those observed in adults
(see section “Undesirable effects”).
The safety and effectiveness of Dawnzera in paediatric patients less than 12 years of age have not

Pharmacokinetics

Absorption

Following subcutaneous administration, donidalorsen is absorbed with the time to maximum plasma
concentration of approximately 2.5 hours post dose, based on population estimates.

Distribution

Donidalorsen is expected to distribute primarily to the liver and kidney cortex after subcutaneous
dosing. The population estimate of apparent volume of distribution for the central (Vc/F) and
peripheral (Vp/F) compartment were 69.8 L and 1840 L, respectively. Donidalorsen is highly bound to
human plasma proteins (>98% bound) in vitro.

Metabolism

Donidalorsen is metabolised by endo- and exonucleases to short oligonucleotide fragments of varying
sizes within the liver. The GalNAc moiety of donidalorsen is cleaved from the ASO portion by rapid
hydrolysis once distributed to tissues.

Elimination

The population estimate of the terminal elimination half-life of the ASO moiety of donidalorsen in a
typical patient with HAE is approximately 1 month. The mean fraction of unchanged ASO eliminated
in urine was less than 1% of the administered dose in healthy subjects within 24 hours. Linker related
metabolites are minimally released to circulation and subsequently rapidly excreted to urine or faeces.

Kinetics in specific patient groups

Population analyses showed no clinically meaningful differences in the pharmacokinetics of
donidalorsen based on sex (79 Male, 98 Female), ethnicity (Black or African American, Caucasian),
geographical region, disease status.
Liver impairment
Population analyses showed no clinically meaningful differences in the pharmacokinetics of
donidalorsen in patient with normal liver function (N=175) or mild hepatic impairment (total bilirubin ≤
1 × ULN and AST > 1 × ULN, or total bilirubin > 1 to 1.5 × ULN and any AST [N=2]). Donidalorsen has
not been studied in patients with moderate or severe hepatic impairment.

Preclinical data

Carcinogenesis
In a 6 month carcinogenicity study in transgenic (Tg.rasH2) mice, subcutaneous administration of
donidalorsen (5, 10, 20, or 60 mg/kg) or a rodent specific surrogate (10 mg/kg) once every 2 weeks
did not result in an increase in malignant tumors. No malignant tumors were found in a 2-year study in
rats. This suggests lack of human carcinogenic risk.

Mutagenesis
Donidalorsen was negative for genotoxicity in in vitro (bacterial reverse mutation, chromosomal
aberration in Chinese hamster lung cells) and in vivo (mouse bone marrow micronucleus) assays.

Pregnancy, lactation and fertility
Subcutaneous administration of donidalorsen (0, 5, 10, or 20 mg/kg/week) or a rodent active inhibitor
of PKK (5 mg/kg/week) to mice every other day throughout pregnancy and weekly throughout
lactation produced no adverse effects on pre- and postnatal development.
In the mouse pre- and postnatal development study, the concentrations of donidalorsen in breast milk
from lactating mice increased in a dose dependent manner at doses ≥10 mg/kg/week, but these
concentrations of donidalorsen in breast milk were >3000 fold lower than the observed tissue
concentrations. Even though donidalorsen was detected in the maternal mouse milk, systemic
exposure in pups was not expected due to the lack of oral absorption of donidalorsen.

Other information

Incompatibilities

In the absence of compatibility studies, this medicinal product must not be mixed with other medicinal
products.

Shelf life

Do not use this medicine after the expiry date marked as “EXP” on the pack.
Dawnzera may be stored in the original carton below 30°C for a single period of up to 6 weeks, but
not beyond the expiry date. In this case, the disposal date must be entered in the designated area on
the original packaging.

Special precautions for storage

Store in a refrigerator (2°C to 8°C).
Keep the pre-filled pen in the outer carton in order to protect from light.
Do not freeze. Do not expose to heat.
Keep out of the reach of children.

Instructions for handling

Prior to initiation, patients and/or caregivers must be trained on proper preparation and administration
of Dawnzera (see Instructions for Use in the Patient Information).
    •   The single-dose pre-filled pen should be removed from the refrigerator 30 minutes prior to the
        injection to reach room temperature. Other warming methods must not be used.
    •   The pre-filled pen must be inspected visually before use. The solution should appear clear and
        colourless to yellow. The pre-filled pen must not be used if cloudiness, particulate matter, or
        discolouration is observed prior to administration.
    •   Dawnzera is to be administered as a subcutaneous injection in the abdomen, upper thigh
        region, or for caregivers only, the back of the upper arm.
    •   Dawnzera must not be injected into areas where the skin is tender, bruised, red, or hard.
