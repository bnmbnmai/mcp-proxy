Statement of Basis and Purpose

This IROD presents the selected interim remedial action for contaminated soil in portions of Operable
Unit (OU) 1 of the Federated Metals Corp Whiting Superfund Site (“FMC Site” or “the Site”). OU1
consists of residential properties, broadly defined as any area with high or unrestricted accessibility
to sensitive populations (e.g., young children less than [<] 7 years old). This interim remedial action
was chosen in accordance with the Comprehensive Environmental Response, Compensation, and
Liability Act of 1980 (CERCLA), 42 USC §§ 9601 et seq., as amended by the Superfund Amendments,
and the National Oil and Hazardous Substances Pollution Contingency Plan (NCP), 40 CFR Part 300, as
amended. The Director of the Superfund & Emergency Management Division of Region 5 has been
delegated the authority to approve this ROD.

This decision is based on the Administrative Record (AR) file for the Site, which has been developed in
accordance with Section 113(k) of CERCLA, and which is available for review at EPA Region 5 Records
Center, 77 West Jackson Boulevard, Chicago, Illinois, and at the following informational repositories:

Assessment of the Site

The response action selected in this ROD is necessary to protect the public health or welfare or the
environment from actual or threatened releases of hazardous substances into the environment.

Description of the Selected Remedy

The IROD sets forth the selected interim remedy for OU1 of the FMC Site. EPA is performing a
remedial investigation and feasibility study to characterize OU1 and develop remedial alternatives for
a final OU1 remedy.


                                                   1
The selected interim remedy in OU1 will address potential exposure to lead-contaminated soil in OU1
by removing that soil from residential properties and disposing of the soil off-Site. The selected
remedy includes the following components:

   1. Excavation and off-Site disposal of soil with a lead concentration exceeding 200 parts per
      million (ppm) to a maximum of 12 inches below the surface.

   2. Placement of a visual barrier at the base of excavation if lead concentration in the soil below
      12 inches exceeds 200 ppm.

   3. Backfilling with clean soil and restoration of vegetation.

   4. Placement and long-term stewardship of institutional controls (ICs) on those properties that
      do not meet the 200 ppm clean up level (CUL) below the base of excavation.

Statutory Determinations

This interim action is protective of human health and the environment in the short term and will
provide adequate protection for the properties addressed until a final ROD is signed; complies with
(or waives) those federal and state requirements that are applicable or relevant and appropriate for
this limited-scope action; and is cost-effective. This action is an interim solution only, and is not
intended to utilize permanent solutions and alternative treatment (or resource recovery)
technologies to the maximum extent practicable or fulfill the statutory preference for remedies that
employ treatment that reduces toxicity, mobility, or volume as a principal element. Because this
remedy will result in hazardous substances remaining on-site above health-based levels, a review will
be conducted to ensure that the remedy continues to provide adequate protection of human health
and the environment within five years after commencement of the remedial action. Subsequent
remedial actions will fully address the threats posed by conditions at the Site.

1      Site Name, Location, and Description

The FMC Site (Superfund Identification Number: IND005444104) includes the approximately 36-acre
footprint of the former FMC smelting facility, and the surrounding neighborhoods in Hammond and
Whiting, Indiana impacted by FMC’s operations (Figure 1). During operations, the FMC facility
generated lead-contaminated waste piles and air emissions that impacted soil on the residential and
non-residential properties in the surrounding neighborhoods in both cities, as well soil on its own
property and the sediments in adjacent George Lake. After FMC ceased operations, other businesses,
including various metals fabricating and reclaiming businesses, have continued to operate at the
former FMC facility.

EPA is the lead agency for the Site, and IDEM serves as the support agency. The selected remedial
action is expected to be funded through federal remedial action funding with associated state cost
share.

Figure 1: Site and Former Facility Location




2      Site History and Enforcement Activities

2.1    Site History

The property on which the FMC facility was located was developed sometime prior to 1930 as a
Federated Metals Corporation plant. In 1938, the facility expanded and was named the Federated

                                                  4
Metals Division of the American Smelting and Refining Company (ASARCO). From 1937 until 1983, the
FMC facility operated as a smelting, refining, recovery, and recycling facility for non-ferrous metals
including copper, zinc and lead. Operations at the former Federated Metals facility included office
work, the storage of raw materials, reclamation of lead dross (impurities that floated on refined lead),
foundry operations, white metal (alloying), a large baghouse (dust collector), oil storage, and a
charcoal briquetting operation. The charcoal was used as a reducing agent for the smelting of certain
ores. During smelting, a flux chemical cleaning agent was used to remove impurities that lead to the
formation of a molten slag. The slag associated with the smelting of lead is high in iron and silica and
once hardened can have a glasslike appearance. Federated Metals was disposing of various wastes
generated during operations, such as the slag, by discharging to the land and/or nearby waterways, in
particular George Lake.

In 1992, FMC and its parent corporation, ASARCO, entered into a federal consent decree to settle an
EPA lawsuit alleging violations of the Resource Conservation and Recovery Act (RCRA) and agreed to
perform a corrective action on the 36-acre property. The RCRA closure and corrective action project,
largely performed from 2001-2006, included demolishing an on-site baghouse; consolidating into the
landfill debris, slag, and on-site hazardous wastes dredged from George Lake; and constructing a
phyto-cap, a remedy that consists of growing plants and trees on a landfill cover to reduce migration
of contaminants into groundwater.

In 2005, before the RCRA corrective action was complete, ASARCO filed for bankruptcy. In 2009, the
bankruptcy court authorized the establishment and funding of a Trust to finish the EPA-required work
at the smelter. The Trust completed the landfill cover, installed groundwater monitoring wells, and
conducted several rounds of groundwater sampling to confirm the landfill cap was preventing
groundwater contamination. Maintenance and monitoring of the landfill cap and groundwater
monitoring to evaluate the groundwater plume continues under EPA’s RCRA authority, but funds for
monitoring may soon be exhausted.

From December 2016 to November 2018, Region 5 Superfund Division conducted a removal site
assessment to assess the impact of atmospheric deposition of lead and arsenic from the FMC facility
and to determine if there was off-site migration of hazardous materials into groundwater. The soil
results showed that some parcels had soil lead concentrations over 1,200 ppm, which was the
threshold for EPA to undertake “time-critical” removal actions. EPA identified 33 properties with lead
concentrations greater than 1,200 ppm that also had sensitive populations – pregnant women or
children under the age of 7 – in residence and performed a time-critical removal action to address
these properties from 2018 to 2019. The cleanup work involved excavating more than 10,000 tons of
lead-contaminated soil, disposing of the soil at an off-site landfill, backfilling excavated areas with
clean soil, and restoring yards to their original condition.

Since August 2021, the city of Hammond has also removed and disposed of lead-contaminated soils
on approximately 60 properties in Hammond with soil lead concentrations greater than 400 ppm,
which was EPA’s threshold for remedial actions at the time. Parcels were generally divided into two
decision units (DUs), one for the back yard and one for the front yard and parkway; some parcels had
only one DU excavated. If the soil concentration of lead was 400 ppm or greater, the DU was
excavated and if the soil concentration of lead was below 400 ppm, no action was taken.

4      Scope and Role of Operable Unit and Response Action

The Site has been divided into two OUs. OU1 consists of residential properties, broadly defined as any
area with high or unrestricted accessibility to sensitive populations (e.g., pregnant women or young
children less than 7 years old), and includes, but is not limited to, properties containing single- and
multi-family dwellings, apartment complexes, vacant lots in residential areas, schools, daycare
centers, community centers, playgrounds, parks and other recreational areas, green ways, and any
other areas where pregnant women or young children may be exposed to site-related contaminated
media. All other Site areas, including the former FMC facility, industrial and commercial properties
within the impacted neighborhoods, and George Lake, are designated as OU2.

EPA’s strategy for cleaning up the Site is to first address unacceptable risks to human health and the
environment posed by lead-contaminated soil on the approximately 160 residential properties with
documented exceedances of the 200 ppm CUL, as an interim remedial action for OU1. After the
remedial investigation for OU1, the final remedial action for OU1 will establish a final CUL and
address soil on additional residential properties that have not yet been sampled. The final remedy
will also address any other contaminants and media in OU1 that EPA finds pose an unacceptable risk
to residents. Non-residential media (e.g., the former FMC facility footprint and sediments in George
Lake) and exposure pathways will be addressed separately as OU2.

This action for residential properties is conducted under EPA’s remedial authority and considered
early and interim because it addresses the known threats to human health. EPA is performing a
remedial investigation that will fully screen contaminants of potential concern (COPCs), define the
extent and magnitude of contamination, and calculate threats to human health and the environment.
The final remedy will be comprehensive and protective for all residential exposures in OU1.

5      Site Characteristics

5.1    Physical Characteristics

The former FMC facility refers to two areas: an approximately 17-acre former smelter area and an
adjacent 19-acre disposal area where the RCRA Corrective Action Management Unit is located (Figure
2). The former facility is within an urbanized area with a mixture of industrial, commercial, and

                                                    6
residential properties as well as vacant lots, community centers, playgrounds, parks, churches and
schools. North of the former facility, a bike path runs along former railroad tracks associated with
FMC’s historic operations, while vacant land and Calumet College of St. Joseph are to the south. The
residential area of concern is comprised of single family and multi-family homes, and other areas
where sensitive populations may be exposed, located primarily north and east of the facility. To the
west, the facility is adjacent to the shore of the 148-acre George Lake. Lake Michigan is approximately
0.7 miles northeast of the Site.

5.2    Geology

The U.S. Department of Agriculture classifies surface soils as “Urban Land” throughout most of the
Site. During the EPA sampling, soil collected from properties around the Site had varying
compositions. The composition for most samples was dark organic, rich soil at the top, with sand,
industrial fill, gravel, clay, or mixed soil below. Hydric marsh soils that frequently create ponds and
drain very poorly are present south of the Site.

The Site lies in the low-lying Calumet Lacustrine Plain within the Northern Moraine and Lake Region.
Unconsolidated deposits are a result of glacial, glaciofluvial, shallow-water coastal and lake, wetland,
and wind-blown sedimentation from the Wisconsinan-age glacial advance and subsequent retreat,
which formed glacial Lake Chicago. Unconsolidated deposits in the Site area are about 50 feet thick
and consist of peat, lake silt and clay, clay-rich till, sand and gravel outwash, and sand beaches and
dunes.

The underlying bedrock at the Site is comprised of more than 4,000 feet of sedimentary rock resting
on a Precambrian granitic basement. The Devonian Antrim Shale lies at the top of the bedrock
                                                    7
sequence, and is underlain by successively older limestone, dolomite, sandstone, and shale units. The
Site lies on the Kankakee Arch, in which consolidated units form a gentle saddle-shaped structure
that dips to the northeast towards the Michigan Basin, and to the southwest to the Illinois Basin.

5.3    Nature and Extent of Contamination

From 2016-2023, EPA collected composite soil samples at 240 residential and non-residential
properties (Figure 3). The main area of sampling was directly north of the former Federated Metals
facility. Most properties were divided into two or more DUs with one or more composite samples
collected from each DU. Of the 240 properties, approximately 215 parcels had concentrations
exceeding 200 ppm, and of those, approximately 160 residential properties have not been
remediated by either EPA or the City of Hammond. An estimated 3800 residential properties are in
the broader area of potential concern (shown as light and dark pink in Figure 1), but these properties
have not been sampled and will be addressed in a future final remedial action for OU1.

Table 1 and Table 2 summarize lead concentrations by depth in soil samples from laboratory analysis
and x-ray florescence (XRF) (a calibrated, field sampling instrument that can detect lead in soil)
analysis, respectively. Very few samples below the 0 to 6 inch interval were analyzed in the
laboratory, so estimates of the extent of contamination below 6 inches rely on the XRF data in Table
2. Generally, exceedances of the 200 ppm screening level are more common in shallower intervals
than deeper intervals, which is consistent with aerial deposition of contamination. The rate of
exceedance of the 200 ppm screening level in the 0 to 6 inch and 6 to 12 inch intervals is 74% and
78%, respectively. However, the exceedance rate drops in subsurface intervals to 56% in the 12 to 18
inch interval, and 34% in the 18 to 24 inch interval. The threats to human health posed by these
concentrations are described in Section 7.

The highest subsurface concentration is associated with a parcel immediately north of the smelting
facility known as “Former Outlot A” and currently owned by the City of Hammond. This parcel may
have received fill or waste from the former facility and is not representative of most residential
properties. The XRF data indicate that median concentrations decrease with depth, but subsurface
soil lead concentrations still generally exceed the 200 ppm screening level. The ubiquity in northwest
Indiana of “urban land”, in which non-native materials have frequently been used as fill, suggests that
lead-contaminated fill may be a contributing source to subsurface concentrations on residential
parcels in OU1 in addition to the aerial deposition pathway. Although fill may have come from
multiple industrial sources in the area, EPA determined through forensic analysis of lead particles
collected from both within the FMC facility and outside of the FMC facility that FMC is largely responsible
for the soil contamination in OU1. EPA’s forensic evaluation of landfill samples and residential soil
samples indicated that the landfill waste, which is known to have been deposited by FMC, and
residential soil samples likely have the same or a similar source. This indicates that the historic FMC
operations (over a period of about 46 years) were likely a substantial source of the lead released to
the soils in the surrounding neighborhoods.

5.4    Principal Threat Waste

The NCP establishes an expectation that EPA will use treatment to address the principal threat posed
by a site wherever practicable. Principal threat wastes are those source materials considered to be
highly toxic or highly mobile, which generally cannot be contained in a reliable manner, or would
present a significant risk to human health or the environment should exposure occur. The NCP (40
CFR 300.430 (a)(1)(iii)(A)) indicates principal threats are most likely to include liquids, areas
contaminated with high concentrations of toxic compounds, and highly mobile materials. Low-level
threat wastes are those source materials that generally can be reliably contained and that would
present only a low risk in the event of release. Low-level threat wastes include source materials that
exhibit low toxicity, low mobility in the environment, or are near health-based levels.

EPA has not identified any principal threat wastes at the Site. Instead, the contaminated soil and the
process residue source materials are considered low-level threat waste materials that can be reliably
contained.

6         Current and Potential Future Site and Resources Uses

The Site is in a heavily urbanized area currently used for residential, recreational, commercial, and
industrial activities. Based on existing use and the zoning ordinances of the cities of Hammond and
Whiting, the current land uses are expected to continue in the future. The current and reasonably
anticipated future land use of the former facility is industrial and commercial, with existing
businesses operating in the former smelter area, and the CAMU located in the disposal area (Figure
2).

In the past several years, the City of Whiting has acquired several properties that are zoned
commercial but that had been in residential use. Those properties are intended for commercial
redevelopment, so while some were sampled as residential in the removal site assessment, their
current and future use is now commercial, and they are not included in this interim remedial action.

Lake Michigan is the source of drinking water for both Hammond and Whiting and all properties are
connected to public water. EPA is not aware of any withdrawal or use of groundwater at the Site.

7         Summary of Site Risks

This early interim action is based on the unacceptable risk to human health from contaminated,
residential soil. EPA will prepare a comprehensive risk assessment and develop final remedial
objectives and CULs in a future final remedial action for OU1.

7.1       Human Health Risk Assessment (HHRA)

The lead contamination in residential soil at the FMC Site presents a threat to human health. Lead
exposure can harm children's developing brains and nervous systems, potentially impacting learning,
behavior, and overall growth. Lead primarily targets the nervous system, kidneys, and blood-forming
(hematopoietic) systems. EPA is using a preliminary remediation goal (PRG) of 200 ppm, for lead in
soil at residential properties in accordance with the October 16, 2025, EPA memorandum Residential
Lead Directive for CERCLA Sites and RCRA Hazardous Waste Cleanup Program Facilities1 (2025 Lead
Directive). As outlined in the 2025 Lead Directive, EPA’s national lead policy specifies the use of a
regional screening level (RSL) of 200 ppm for lead in residential soil and a target children’s blood lead
level (BLL) of 5 micrograms per deciliter (µg/dL) to develop PRGs.

EPA uses the Integrated Exposure Uptake Biokinetic (IEUBK) Model for Lead in Children as a risk
assessment tool to support environmental cleanup decisions at residential sites. The IEUBK Model
considers exposure to lead from several sources and routes, and correlates BLLs in young children
(birth to 7 years of age) to lead concentrations in environmental media. EPA used IEUBKv2 to
calculate a new risk-based PRG for lead that relies on default values for the purposes of this interim
remedy. The results indicate that the Site-specific interim risk-based residential soil PRG is 200 ppm.

Consistent with the 2025 Lead Directive, the risk-based PRG may be adjusted based on ARARs or to
account for uncertainty, technical limitations (i.e., detection and/or quantification limits),

bioavailability and site-specific soil lead background. Considering the applicability of such factors, the
resulting site-specific CUL for lead in residential soil at OU1 is 200 ppm.

Residential exposure to contaminants in soil occurs through incidental ingestion, inhalation, and
dermal contact (collectively, “direct contact”). According to the Agency for Toxic Substances and
Disease Registry (ATSDR), the public is generally exposed to only the top few inches of soil on a
regular basis. ATSDR defines surface soil as the top 0 to 3 inches.2 With respect to risk assessment,
the top inch of soil best represents current direct contact exposure to contaminants. As shown in
Table 1, at least 88% of the shallowest (0-3 inches and 0-6 inches) residential composite samples
collected by EPA and analyzed by laboratory methods exceeded the screening level of 200 ppm for
lead. The maximum lead concentration at these surficial depths was 3,110 ppm, which the IEUBK
model correlates to a BLL of 14.5 μg/dL. This level indicates that the lead from the Site now in
residential soils is a significant potential threat to the health of child residents. Lead serves as the sole
basis for taking this interim remedial action to address residential soil at the Site. Lead is the only
COPC for this interim action.

While the top one to three inches of soil is the most significant source for current exposure to
contaminants, exposure to contaminants in soil below the top inch may still occur over the long term.
Soil as deep as 12 inches below grade can be frequently brought to the surface through typical
residential activities such as gardening, landscaping, and digging by small children and pets. This is
consistent with guidance from ATSDR that exposures from subsurface soil could occur from activities
such as gardening, digging, and construction.3 As shown in Table 2, 78% of the properties sampled by
XRF exceeded the 200 ppm screening level for lead in the 6 to 12-inch interval, which indicates
significant lead contamination to this depth.

XRF analyses of samples below 12 inches below grade showed exceedances of the 200 ppm screening
level in 56% of the parcels at the 12 to 18-inch interval, and 34% of the parcels at the 18 to 24-inch
interval (see Table 2). These analyses indicate decreasing but still widespread elevated lead
concentrations in subsurface soil. At-grade vegetable gardening is a residential activity that could
cause exposure to soil in the 12 to 24-inch interval. Other residential activities that disturb soil at this
depth, such as the planting of shrubs and trees with very large root balls, excavating for an inground
pool or underground utilities, and excavation of a basement for new construction, are infrequent.
These types of activities are rarely performed by residents, and are not performed by children, who
are the most vulnerable to health impacts from lead. Although these subsurface soil disturbances
could result in mixing of subsurface soil with surface soil, the relatively infrequent nature and low
volumes of subsurface soil brought to the surface supports the conclusion that there is a substantially
lower residential exposure to subsurface soil than there is residential exposure to the 0 to 12-inch
interval.

8         Remedial Action Objective

Because of the unacceptable potential risks to human health and the environment described in
Section 7, the Remedial Action Objective (RAO) for this IROD is:

      •   Reduce to acceptable levels the human health risk from exposure to lead in soil through
          ingestion, direct contact, or inhalation exposure pathways, assuming residential land-use
          scenarios.

The properties to be addressed in this interim remedial action are generally either currently in
residential use or are vacant but zoned residential and suitable for residential use in the future. EPA
also applies residential exposure assumptions to other areas with high or unrestricted accessibility to
sensitive populations, such as schools, daycare centers, community centers, playgrounds, parks, and
other areas where young children may be exposed to contaminated soil.

8.1       Clean Up Level

CULs specify the chemical concentration for achieving the RAO. EPA is selecting a lead concentration
of 200 ppm as the surface soil CUL for this interim action. As described in Section 7.1, EPA has
identified a target BLL of 5 µg/dL as the appropriate protective BLL endpoint for remediation at the
FMC Site. Using default inputs to the IEUBK model, this target BLL for lead corresponds to a soil lead
concentration of 200 ppm. Selecting a CUL of 200 ppm for this interim remedy will allow EPA to
quickly address residential properties with known high levels of lead.

While the interim remedy is being performed, EPA will also be collecting information on background
concentrations and bioavailability of lead, as described below, to calculate a final CUL. EPA will
identify the final RAO and soil CUL in a final OU1 Record of Decision. However, the fundamental
remedial approach of excavating lead-contaminated surface soil and using institutional controls (ICs)
with ongoing outreach to notify future homeowners of contamination remaining in the subsurface, is
not expected to change. Therefore, this interim remedy is consistent with the expected final remedy.

The 200 ppm interim CUL is greater than the Site-specific background concentration of 110 ppm
calculated by IDEM in its Expanded Site Inspection Report. To further refine the background
concentration, EPA is collecting, as part of the remedial investigation for OU1, additional background
samples and will refine the Site-specific background lead concentration before selecting a final CUL
for lead. EPA does not expect the final background lead concentration to exceed 200 ppm.

The 200 ppm interim CUL also assumes a default value of 60% for relative bioavailability. Relative
bioavailability measures how much of the lead in the soil is available for absorption by the body. EPA
has not completed the study of the bioavailability of lead in Site soils, and the actual Site-specific
value may be less than or greater than the default value. However, the range of lead bioavailability
seen previously in Region 5 soils suggests that the Site-specific value is likely to differ from the default
value by less than 10%. A difference of less than 10% between the Site-specific value and the default

                                                    13
value is not likely to result in a significant change to the target lead concentration. Selection and
application of a final surface soil CUL may incorporate the Site-specific bioavailability value
determined during the remedial investigation.

10       Comparative Analysis of Alternatives

Section 121 of CERCLA establishes statutory requirements and preferences for remedy selection,
including:

     •   Overall protection of human health and the environment;

     •   Compliance of the remedial action with all federal and more stringent state and tribal
         environmental and facility siting standards, requirements, criteria, or limitations, unless a
         waiver is invoked;

     •   Cost-effectiveness;

     •   Utilization of permanent solutions and alternative treatment technologies or resource
         recovery technologies to the maximum extent practicable; and

     •   A preference for remedies in which treatment that permanently and significantly reduces the
         volume, toxicity, or mobility of the hazardous substances is a principal element, as opposed to
         remedies not involving such treatment.

Building upon these statutory mandates, the NCP articulates nine evaluation criteria for assessing and
comparing the remedial alternatives. The nine criteria fall into three groups: threshold criteria,
primary balancing criteria, and modifying criteria. Threshold criteria, including overall protection of
human health and the environment and compliance with ARARs, must be met for a remedial

among alternatives and include: long-term effectiveness and permanence; reduction of toxicity,

10.1   Overall Protection of Human Health and the Environment

This criterion assesses how well the alternatives achieve and maintain protection of human health
and the environment. It draws on the assessments conducted under other evaluation criteria,
especially long-term effectiveness, short-term effectiveness, and permanence.

Alternative 1 would provide no risk reduction and would not be protective of human health and the
environment. Because it does not meet this threshold criterion, it is not eligible for selection and was
not further considered with respect to the other selection criteria.

Alternatives 2 and 3 provide overall protectiveness of human health and the environment by
removing surface soil from residential properties and preventing exposure to contaminated soil
below the depth of excavation. The long-term protectiveness of both alternatives relies on ICs and
the integrity of the clean soil cover. Both alternatives have some risk of impacts to the local
community from traffic and dust created during the remedial action, but these risks are manageable.

10.2   Compliance with ARARs

This criterion addresses whether the remedial alternatives comply with promulgated federal and
state applicable or relevant and appropriate requirements (ARARs). Only state requirements that are
more stringent than federal requirements are ARARs. There are three different categories of ARARs:
chemical-specific, action-specific, and location-specific ARARs. “To Be Considered” (TBC)
requirements are non-promulgated criteria, advisories, etc., that may be useful to determine what is
protective or in developing Superfund remedies.

The potential ARARs and TBCs are identified in Attachment 1. There are no chemical-specific ARARs;
rather, the RAO and CUL for this action were developed with consideration of guidance in the 2025
Lead Directive. The active remedial alternatives (2 and 3) would have action- and location-specific
ARARs, such as requirements related to hazardous waste determination, management,
transportation and disposal; air emissions and fugitive dust; surface water discharge from the work
site; and institutional controls. Although interim remedies are not required by statute or regulation to
comply with ARARs, both Alternatives 2 and 3 would comply with ARARs listed in Attachment 1.

10.3   Long-term Effectiveness and Permanence

The factors for this criterion include the magnitude of risk associated with residual contamination
after the conclusion of remedial activities, and the adequacy and reliability of the remedy.
Alternatives 2 and 3 would both reduce the risks from exposure to lead-contaminated soil by
removing surface soil from the site and safely disposing of it, and by backfilling properties with clean
soil.

drainage of stormwater away from structures. Because the yards in this dense urban area are

10.6   Implementability

This criterion considers the technical and administrative feasibility of a remedy. Assessing the ease or
difficulty of implementing an alternative may include: (1) its technical feasibility or challenges
associated with its construction and operation, the ease of undertaking additional actions, and the
ability to monitor the remedy; (2) its administrative feasibility or non-technical challenges to
implementation, such as the need for approvals or permits and the associated time and effort; (3) the
availability of technical services and materials; and (4) the availability of necessary equipment or
other resources.

Both Alternative 2 and Alternative 3 utilize a routine and widely available technology – excavation
and offsite disposal – and can be implemented. The larger volume of soil that would be excavated in
Alternative 3 may slightly increase the difficulty of implementation compared with Alternative 2.
However, a larger number of property owners who must agree to implement ICs on their properties
in Alternative 2 may slightly increase the difficulty of fully implementing the remedy of Alternative 2.

The implementability of Alternative 2 would be limited by the need to slope the ground surface on
parcels with raised surface elevations. Raising the grade by six inches would impact stormwater
drainage on the remediated properties as well as neighboring properties. At minimum, Alternative 2
would require additional design considerations to ensure proper drainage of stormwater, and
additional monitoring of erosion may be needed. However, small and fragmented areas that are
targeted for remediation may not be able to have the grade raised enough to meet the 12-inch clean
soil requirement. Therefore, Alternative 2 partially meets the implementability criterion, while
Alternative 3 fully meets the implementability criterion.

treat large volumes of soil containing relatively low concentrations of lead.

13.1   Protection of Human Health and the Environment

The selected remedy will protect human health and the environment by reducing residential
exposure to lead-contaminated soil. It will achieve this by removing contaminated soil to a depth of
12 inches and disposing of contaminated soil in a permitted off-site landfill and using engineering
controls and ICs to prevent exposure to contaminated subsurface soil. The selected remedy will result
in a protective target children’s BLL of 5 μg/dL or less. The remedy will comply with ARARs and
implementation will not pose any unacceptable short-term risks.

13.2   Compliance with Applicable or Relevant and Appropriate Requirements

The selected remedy will comply with all federal and more stringent state ARARs that pertain to the
remedial action at the Site. The ARARs for the selected remedy are listed and discussed in detail in
the tables in Attachment 1 to this ROD, and include requirements related to hazardous waste
determination, management, transportation and disposal; air emissions and fugitive dust; water
discharge; and institutional controls.

13.3   Cost-Effectiveness

EPA makes this determination by comparing the relative advantages and disadvantages of the
alternatives with respect to long-term protectiveness, short-term protectiveness, reduction of
toxicity, mobility, or volume through treatment, and cost. EPA finds a remedy to be cost-effective if
its costs are proportional to its overall effectiveness. Alternative 3 has the best overall effectiveness
of the alternatives because the 12-inch thickness of clean soil would be achieved over all accessible
areas, permanently reducing exposure to lead-contaminated soil, while only marginally increasing the
potential negative short-term impacts to the community. This greater overall effectiveness justifies
the increased cost of Alternative 3 over Alternative 2, so Alternative 3 is a cost-effective remedy.

13.4   Utilization of Permanent Solutions and Treatment or Recovery to the Maximum Extent
       Practicable

EPA has determined that Alternative 3 represents the maximum extent to which permanent solutions
and treatment technologies can be utilized in a practicable manner at the Site. There are no
demonstrated technologies to treat lead-contaminated soil for the purpose of reducing residential
exposure on the scale needed for this remedy, but excavation, removal and disposal of soil in a
permitted landfill will permanently eliminate residential exposure to the lead in the surface soil.

13.5   Preference for Treatment as a Principal Element

None of the soil addressed in this remedial action constitutes principal threat waste at the site, so the
preference for treatment of principal threat waste does not apply.

13.6   Five-Year Review Requirements

Because this remedy will result in hazardous substances remaining on-site above levels that allow for
unlimited use and unrestricted exposure, a statutory review will be conducted within five years after
initiation of the remedial action to ensure that the remedy is, or will be, protective of human health
and the environment. The purpose of the five-year reviews is to evaluate the implementation and
performance of the remedy in order to determine if the remedy is or will be protective of human
health and the environment. The five-year review will document recommendations and follow-up
actions as necessary to ensure long-term protectiveness of the remedy or bring about protectiveness
of a remedy that is not protective.

Comment 13: The Proposed Plan sets a target for children’s BLL that is inconsistent with the 3.5
            μg/dL blood lead reference value (BLRV) established by the U.S. Centers for Disease

EPA Response:

EPA acknowledges the difference between the CDC BLRV of 3.5 µg/dL and the 5 µg/dL target
BLL recommended in the 2025 Residential Lead Directive. According to the CDC, the BLRV is a
population-based measurement indicating that 2.5% of U.S. children aged 1–5 years have BLLs at or
above 3.5 μg/dL. It is a screening tool to identify children with higher levels of lead in their blood
compared with levels in most children, it changes over time as the population’s BLL distribution
changes, and it is not a health-based standard or a toxicity threshold.1 In contrast, EPA is selecting a
cleanup under CERLCA to remediate potentially unacceptable threats to human health based on a
predictive model, and without collecting causal evidence of elevated BLLs. Therefore, the use and
purpose of the CDC BLRV differ from the use and purpose of BLL for CERCLA cleanup decision‑making.

EPA is using the 2025 Directive’s nationally-established target BLL to derive PRGs and CULs under
CERCLA. The 5 µg/dL target provides a consistent modeling endpoint for PRG development across
EPA’s cleanup programs and supports timely decision making. EPA will continue to evaluate exposure
scenarios, updated scientific information, and Site-specific conditions during the remedial
investigation for OU1. The final ROD for OU1 may add or modify PRGs, as appropriate.
