I. INTRODUCTION

The purpose of a Five-Year Review (FYR) is to evaluate the implementation and performance of a
remedy in order to determine if the remedy is and will continue to be protective of human health and
the environment. The methods, findings, and conclusions of reviews are documented in five-year
review reports such as this one. In addition, FYR reports identify issues found during the review, if any,
and document recommendations to address them.

The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency is preparing this FYR pursuant to the Comprehensive
Environmental Response, Compensation, and Liability Act (CERCLA) Section 121, consistent with the
National Contingency Plan (NCP) 40 Code of Federal Regulation (CFR) Section 300.430(f)(4)(ii) and
considering EPA policy.

This is the fifth FYR for the Cape Fear Wood Preserving Site. The triggering action for this policy review
is the previous FYR report dated September 30, 2021. The FYR has been prepared because hazardous
substances, pollutants, or contaminants remain at the Site above levels that allow for unlimited use
and unrestricted exposure (UU/UE). The Site consists of one operable unit (OU), which addresses both
soil and groundwater contamination.

The EPA Remedial Project Manager (RPM) Susan Kibler led the FYR. Participants included the EPA
Community Involvement Coordinator Brenda Bonner, and the North Carolina Department of
Environmental Quality (NCDEQ) FYR preparer Ben Ammerman. The review began on 10/28/2025.
Documents used to prepare this FYR are summarized in Appendix A. Appendix B provides a brief site
chronology. Appendix E includes site status information.

Site Background
The Cape Fear Wood Preserving Superfund Site (Site) is located at 1219 South Reilly Road, Fayetteville,
Cumberland County, North Carolina. The Site consists of 10 developed acres within a 41-acre tract, the
remainder of which is wooded. A single one-story utility building currently stands on Site. A perimeter
fence composed of chain link and topped by barbed wire surrounds the entire 41-acre property. A
railroad right-of-way runs between the northwest perimeter fence and Reilly Road. Property access is
via two locked gates along this perimeter fence. A small stream flows southeast on the property, in
parallel proximity to the southwest property line, and a small wetland area is located to the northeast.
See figures in Appendix D for site location maps. The current land use remains unchanged, and
groundwater is not being used as a potable water source.

Cape Fear Wood Preserving began operations at the Site in 1953 and continued until 1983. The facility
produced creosote-treated wood from 1953 until 1978 when demand for creosote-treated products
declined. A Wolmanizing process using salts containing sodium dichromate, copper sulfate, and arsenic
pentoxide or otherwise known as the Chromated Copper Arsenate (CCA) treatment process was used
to treat the wood. The date the CCA process was initiated is unknown, as is whether the creosote and
CCA process occurred simultaneously or in succession. Both liquid and sludge wastes were generated
by these two treatment processes. Wastes from the creosote process were pumped into a concrete
sump north of the treatment unit. Liquid separated from the sludge was pumped into a drainage ditch
southeast of the developed portion of the Site and discharged into a diked pond. Storm water run-off

from the treatment yard also drained to this ditch. Waste from the CCA treatment process was
pumped into an unlined lagoon north of the dry kiln and allowed to percolate into the ground.

II. RESPONSE ACTION SUMMARY

Basis for Taking Action

The 1989 Record of Decision (ROD) identified the following Contaminants of Concern (COCs):
       Media                                      Constituents of Concern
 Groundwater          Benzene; PAHs (carcinogenic); PAHs (noncarcinogenic)
 Surface water        Arsenic; chromium (total); copper
 Soil                 Arsenic; benzene; chromium (total); PAHs (carcinogenic); PAHs (total)
 Sediment             PAH (total); arsenic; chromium (total)

These exposure pathways were evaluated under the current land use conditions of undeveloped with
minimal industrialization:

       •   Direct contact with contaminated surface soils by children trespassing on the Site,
       •   Inhalation of fugitive dust originating from contaminated soil areas by site trespassers and
           nearby residents, and
       •   Contact with contaminated sediments by children wading on-site in the diked pond and
           drainage ditch.

Additional human exposure pathways were evaluated should the future use of the Site and
surrounding area become either more industrial or residentially oriented. These additional exposure
pathways are:

       •   Direct contact with contaminated surface soils by future residents and workers;
       •   Inhalation of fugitive dust originating from contaminated soil areas by future workers; and
       •   Ingestion of groundwater from the upper and lower aquifers.

The 2001 ROD Amendment stated in the Assessment of the Site section that no unacceptable current
risks had been identified with the Cape Fear Site; the principle threat, therefore, pertained to the
potential future use of the groundwater beneath and downgradient of the Site as a source of potable
water. Additionally, the 2001 ROD Amendment stated that the threat posed by soils to either public
health or the environment was eliminated when approximately 113,000 cubic yards of contaminated
soils were thermally treated at the Site between June 1998 and May 1999. The 2001 amended remedy
addressed the future unacceptable risks posed by the contaminated groundwater at the Site.

Response Actions
Summary of Pre-ROD Activities
Pre-ROD Actions
The EPA conducted an emergency removal action at the Site during January and February 1985. This
action included: removal of creosote sludge from the creosote concrete sump; removal of sludge from
the lagoon to a depth of seven feet and solidification of the sludge with fly ash; pumping of lagoon
water into storage tanks south of the new CCA unit; removal of contaminated soil from the drainage
ditch parallel to the railroad tracks and at an intersecting culvert; removal of contaminated soil from a

                                                    6
portion of the northeast swamp and stained areas in the treatment yard; and backfilling of excavation
with clean, sandy soil.

In September 1986, the EPA conducted a second emergency response action, when site visits revealed
that vandals had shot holes in a 3,000- gallon creosote storage tank, spilling approximately 500 gallons
of creosote onto the ground. The cleanup action included: removal, solidification, and transport of
approximately 10 cubic yards of creosote contaminated sludge to an on-site metal shed east of the
new CCA unit; removal and transport of the creosote storage tanks to the on-site metal shed;
excavation and grading of the area where the release had occurred; pumping of approximately
15,000 gallons of the CCA waste water from the CCA recovery sump into the on-site storage tanks
south of the new CCA unit; and containment of the CCA recovery sump within an earthen dike.

The EPA proposed listing the Site on the Superfund program's National Priorities List (NPL) on
June 10, 1986. The EPA finalized the Site’s listing on the NPL on July 22, 1987.

Remedy Components
1989 ROD
The EPA issued a ROD for the site on June 30, 1989. The following clean-up objectives were determined
based on regulatory requirements and levels of contamination found at the Site:
       • To protect the public health and the environment from exposure to contaminated
           on-site soils through inhalation, direct contact, and erosion of soils into surface waters
           and wetlands,
       • To prevent off-site movement of contaminated groundwater, and
       • To restore contaminated groundwater to levels protective of human health and
           the environment.
The major components of the selected remedy included:
       • Remediation of Hazardous Materials, Tanks, and Piping via dismantling, removal, and
           off-site disposal of all on-site wastes and contaminated structures,
       • Source Control (Remediation of Contaminated Soils) through excavation and on-site
           treatment of contaminated surface and subsurface soils, and Migration Control
           (Remediation of Contaminated Groundwater) via groundwater extraction, onsite treatment,
           and discharge.

1991 Explanation of Significant Differences (ESD)
The EPA modified the selected remedy by issuing an ESD on September 24, 1991. The modifications
were based on information generated as part of the treatability studies conducted as part of the
1989-1990 Remedial Design (RD). The issuance of this ESD was warranted for the following reasons:
       • the selection of soil washing over low thermal desorption as the primary technology to
          address soil contamination at the Site;
       • the possible need for solidification of some soil using a cement/fly ash mixture to address
          the elevated concentrations of the metals, arsenic and chromium;
       • the selection of activated carbon adsorption as the primary treatment technology for all
          contaminated water
       • the potential need for pretreatment of the contaminated water stream to remove
          suspended solids and oxidized iron prior to activated carbon filtration; and
       • the selection of Bones Creek as the discharge point for the treated water.

1995 ESD
The EPA modified the selected remedy again by issuing a second ESD on August 14, 1995. This ESD was
required to discharge treated water into the drainage ditch on the southeast side of the Site as
                                                                9
activities conducted during the early phase of the remedial action (RA) generated small amounts of
contaminated water. Since the discharge pipeline would not be installed until later in the RA, the
contaminated water generated was treated and discharged on site. The water discharged on site was
treated using activated carbon to meet the substantive requirements of a National Pollutant Discharge
Elimination System (NPDES) permit.

1996 ESD
The EPA modified the selected remedy again by issuing a third ESD on May 31, 1996. This modification:
       • Eliminated the biotreatment step of the slurry from the soil remediation process, and
       • Changed the point of discharge of the treated water from Bones Creek to the local Publicly
         Owned Treatment Works (POTW).

2001 ROD Amendment
The EPA modified the selected remedy by issuing a ROD Amendment on March 23, 2001. No
changes were made to the remedial action objectives. This ROD Amendment modified the
groundwater remedy to:
      • Extract contaminated groundwater and Dense Non-Aqueous Phase Liquid (DNAPL) through
          recovery (extraction) wells and the French Drain located within the boundaries of the plume.
      • Treat extracted groundwater to levels necessary for discharge.
      • Amend treated water with nutrients to promote in-situ biodegradation of contaminants.
      • Discharge amended water back into the aquifer through infiltration galleries located within
          and at the boundaries of the plume and if necessary, discharge treated groundwater to the
          local POTW.
      • Inject ambient air (air sparging) into saturated soils through air sparge wells distributed
          throughout the dissolved phase of the plume.
      • Install additional monitoring wells.
      • Monitored natural attenuation in the shallow and deeper aquifers.

The 2001 ROD Amendment revised the groundwater cleanup goals to reflect the updated
North Carolina groundwater requirements in the North Carolina Classifications and Water Quality
Standards, Subchapter 2L North Carolina Administrative Code (NC 2L).

As noted above, regarding soils, the 2001 ROD Amendment included a statement that, “The threat
posed by soils to either public health or the environment was eliminated when approximately
113,000 cubic yards of contaminated soils were thermally treated at the Site between June 1998 and
May 1999." (See Phase III implementation section below for additional details. A comprehensive
discussion of the soil treatment including depths and extent of the excavation can be found in the
1999 Remedial Action Completion Report.)

2022 ROD Amendment
The EPA modified the 2001 remedy by issuing a ROD Amendment on September 22, 2022. In addition
to modifying the remedy, the 2022 ROD Amendment revised the groundwater cleanup goals to reflect
the April 1, 2022, North Carolina groundwater requirements the North Carolina Classifications and
Water Quality Standards, Subchapter 2L North Carolina Administrative Code (NC 2L).

The 2022 ROD amendment modified the remedy to:
      • In-situ solidification/stabilization treatment of DNAPL in the Main Source Area and
         Secondary Source Area.
      • Construction of a composite cap over the treated areas that complies with Resource
         Conservation and Recovery Act (RCRA) applicable or relevant and appropriate
         requirements (ARARs).
      • Installation of grout walls perpendicular to the railroad to alleviate the need for
         track replacement.
      • Enhanced in-situ bioremediation: injection of oxygen into the aquifer via underground
         injection wells, to enhance the rate of natural degradation of contaminants in groundwater.
      • Long-term cap maintenance and groundwater monitoring.
      • Implementation of institutional controls (ICs) to prevent well installation and use of
         contaminated groundwater, to provide increased public awareness, and to restrict
         disturbance of in-situ treated waste that remains at the Site and interference with other
         remedy components.

The 2022 ROD Amendment also added four chemicals of concern: styrene, benzo(g,h,i)perylene,
2-methylnaphthalene, and carbazole (diphenylamine) as well as changed several cleanup levels to be

Status of Implementation
The EPA issued the RA Work Assignment to Bechtel Environmental, Inc., in September 1994. The RA
was divided into four phases.



                                                      12
Phase I
Phase I work was conducted from July through September 1995. Activities included:
        • Clearing the Site;
        • Installing an access control fence around the entire Site;
        • Emptying, flushing, cleaning, and off-site disposal of nine storage tanks and
          associated piping;
        • Treating contaminated water (surface water, storage tank liquids, rinse water, water from
          dewatering excavation, etc.) by means of a temporary treatment facility; and
        • Transporting and disposing of debris, hazardous waste material and asbestos-containing
          insulation to either a municipal landfill or a RCRA-permitted hazardous waste landfill
          (as appropriate).

Phase II
Phase II was conducted from December 1995 through February 1996. This phase involved temporarily
relocating the existing railroad track and then restoring the railroad track following remediation of the
underlying contaminated soils and removing the spur.

Phase III
Phase III was conducted from February 1995 through May 1996. Phase III included:
        • Installing the discharge pipeline to the POTW;
        • Dismantling and disposing of building structures;
        • Excavating and treating contaminated soils;
        • Treating and discharging contaminated water; and
        • Backfilling and restoring disturbed areas.
The soil remediation was subdivided into two phases: Phase IIIA and Phase IIIB. Phase IIIA involved
implementing a soil washing technology. In 1996, the EPA concluded that the soil washing process did
not achieve the ROD’s soil performance standards. Consequently, the EPA initiated low-temperature
thermal desorption which was the alternate source control alternative for soils specified in the 1989
ROD. The treatment of the soils via low-temperature thermal desorption was designated as Phase IIIB.
Treatment of contaminated soil was completed from July 1998 through May 1999. Approximately
113,000 cubic yards of contaminated soils were eventually excavated, thermally treated, backfilled,
graded, and re-vegetated. In the bottom of the largest excavation in the middle of the Site, a
groundwater extraction trench, commonly known as a French drain, was installed prior to backfilling
this excavation with treated soils. The drain itself is 80 feet long and approximately 25 feet below
grade. The two primary goals for installing this drain were to enhance the removal of DNAPL
emanating from the west-northwest and the west-southwest side walls of the excavation and allow the
elimination of a significant number of extraction wells called for in the 1990 RD. The soil remediation
phase did not pursue removing this DNAPL as it was below the water table and the EPA elected to deal
with the DNAPL along with groundwater.

Phase IV
Phase IV was conducted from February 1995 through September 1996. Phase IV included:
       • Installing groundwater extraction wells, monitoring wells, and piezometers;
       • Constructing a groundwater treatment plant;
                                                   13
       • Installing a groundwater discharge system; and
       • Operating and maintaining the groundwater treatment plant.
The groundwater remediation system included seven recovery wells, the French Drain, 12 air sparging
wells, 10 infiltration galleries, 35 monitoring wells, and the treatment building. Inside the treatment
building is an oil-water separator, a 10,000-gallon equalization tank, bag filters, two granular activated
carbon filters, two air compressors, and all the controls for these systems. Three of the recovery wells
and the French Drain have two pumps, one to remove contaminated groundwater and establish
hydraulic control and one to capture DNAPL. On July 1, 2002, the EPA and NC DENR concurred that the
groundwater remedy was operational and functional.

Additional investigation in 2009 estimated that approximately 25,000 cubic yards of soil requiring
treatment remained on-site. The EPA determined that a more aggressive remedial approach was
warranted. For monitored natural attenuation (MNA) to be a feasible long-term treatment alternative,
the mass of NAPL and the adsorbed phase soil contamination needed to be reduced so that leachate
from the source area does not serve as a significant continuing source of COC impacts to groundwater.

2019 System Shutdown and Sampling Events
In March 2019, the NCDEQ petitioned the EPA to shut down the pump and treat system at the Site due
to excessive operational cost and minimal impact on plume contaminant reduction. Concurrence was
provided by the EPA, and the system was shut down on March 15, 2019. In March 2019, NCDEQ also
sampled 21 monitoring wells to act as a baseline monitoring event. The State continued to sample the
monitoring wells every six months for two years to analyze the effects of shutting down the system
and annually beginning with the Spring 2022 sampling event. During this five-year review period four
sampling events occurred. Appendix I includes a copy of the 2025 NC DEQ Monitored Natural
Attenuation Operations & Maintenance Monitoring Well Sampling Report.

The remedy selected by the 2022 ROD Amendment is currently in the design phase with construction
expected to start in 2030.

2024 Pump and Treat System Decommissioning
Following the shutdown of the pump and treatment system in 2019 and the issuance of the 2022 ROD
Amendment, NCDEQ requested concurrence from the EPA to decommission the pump and treat
system and the EPA provided concurrence on January 5, 2024, via email. In December 2024, the
decommissioning was completed (see Appendix I for the full decommissioning plan).
Groundwater monitoring of the MNA network is continuing, and a data review is provided in the
Data Review section.

INSTITUTIONAL CONTROLS (ICS) REVIEW
ICs are defined as non-engineered instruments, such as administrative and legal controls, that help
minimize the potential for human exposure to contamination and/or protect the integrity of the
remedy. The 2022 AROD required institutional controls to prevent land and groundwater use and
interference with remedial components, long-term cap maintenance, and groundwater monitoring.
Groundwater is not currently being used as a potable water source, but as called for in the decision
documents, institutional controls are needed to prevent access to groundwater until remediation goals

Well Construction Act) prohibit installing drinking water wells into contaminated aquifers. These
regulations pertain to the permitting and installation of water supply wells and the role of the local
health departments as the permitting authority.

V. TECHNICAL ASSESSMENT

Question A: Is the remedy functioning as intended by the decision documents?
Question A Summary:

No. The pump and treat remedy, as stated in the 2001 ROD Amendment, is not functioning as designed
and implemented. The pump and treat system was shut down in 2019 and decommissioned in 2024;
however, groundwater monitoring, as required by the 2001 ROD Amendment, is still being conducted
to assess the impact on COCs in groundwater. During the FYR reporting period, COC concentrations
increased at the western edge of the groundwater plume, an indication that the plume may be
migrating and therefore not stable. The EPA determined that a more aggressive remedial approach
                                                 19
was warranted to allow the Site to transition faster to MNA and to reduce the overall risk to the
community and environment. In 2022, a ROD Amendment was issued providing for a more aggressive
remedy approach that will ensure long-term protection of the public and environment as well as being
more cost effective. The 2022 ROD Amendment, which addresses two DNAPL source areas and the
dilute groundwater plume, is currently in the design phase and scheduled to be constructed in 2030.

Currently, no human exposure pathways exist to contaminated soil or groundwater. The soil
contamination at the Cape Fear Site was remediated through source removal using thermal treatment.
Groundwater is not currently being used as a potable water source, but as called for in the decision
documents, ICs are needed to prevent access to groundwater until remediation goals are met.
Governmental controls based on state regulations, such as 15A NCAC 02C .0101 (NC Well Construction
Act), are in place to prohibit installing drinking water wells into contaminated aquifers. These
regulations pertain to the permitting and installation of water supply wells and the role of the local
health departments as the permitting authority. Additional ICs to prevent disturbance of in-situ treated
waste will be implemented after In-situ solidification/stabilization has been completed as required by
the 2022 ROD amendment.

Question B: Are the exposure assumptions, toxicity data, clean-up levels and remedial action
objectives (RAOs) used at the time of the remedy still valid?
Question B Summary:

Yes. The current land use remains unchanged, and groundwater is not being used as a potable water
source. The cleanup levels were updated in the 2022 ROD Amendment to reflect the April 1, 2022,
changes to the NC Classifications and Water Quality Standards applicable to NC 2L Standards on which
several of the remedial goals are based. The NC Classifications and Water Quality Standards applicable
to NC 2L Standards were last amended on November 1, 2025; however, none of the ROD cleanup
levels in the 2022 ROD amendment changed.

Table 3 provides a comparison of remediation goals and current Federal and State Standards. As shown
in Table 3 and Appendix F, the NC 2L standard is nominally more or less stringent than 2001 ROD
Amendment and the current remediation goal for several COCs, where it appears that the NC 2L
standards were rounded to one significant figure.

Vapor Intrusion
The EPA’s 2021 Five-Year Review Report included a screening-level evaluation of the vapor intrusion
pathway. Based on results of the evaluation, the EPA determined that there was not an unacceptable
risk to human health. It is the EPA’s expectation that the Amended Remedy will greatly reduce or
eliminate any source of vapors beneath the Site. There are no occupied structures onsite.

                                      APPENDIX F – ARAR REVIEW



Section 121 (d)(2)(A) of CERCLA specifies that Superfund remedial actions must meet any federal
standards, requirements, criteria, or limitations that are determined to be legally ARARs. Applicable or
Relevant and Appropriate Requirements are those standards, criteria, or limitations promulgated
under federal or state law that specifically address a hazardous substance, pollutant, contaminant,
action, location, or other circumstance at a CERCLA site. To-Be-Considered criteria (TBCs) are
non-promulgated advisories and guidance that are not legally binding but should be considered in
determining the necessary level of cleanup for protection of human health or the environment. While
TBCs do not have the status of ARARs, the EPA’s approach to determining if a remedial action is
protective of human health and the environment involves consideration of TBCs along with ARARs.
Chemical-specific ARARs are specific numerical quantity restrictions on individually listed contaminants
in specific media. Examples of chemical-specific ARARs include the MCLs specified under the Safe
Drinking Water Act (SDWA) as well as the ambient water quality criteria that are enumerated under
the Clean Water Act. Because there are usually numerous contaminants of potential concern for any
site, various numerical quantity requirements can be ARARs. In performing the Five-Year Review for
compliance with ARARs, only those ARARs that address the protectiveness of the remedy are reviewed.
Because the remedy at the Site currently addresses only groundwater contamination, this Five-Year
Review will discuss compliance with chemical-specific groundwater ARARs only.

CURRENT POTENTIALLY APPLICABLE ARARS
It is the EPA’s policy that ARARs are generally “frozen” at the time of the ROD signature unless a “new
or modified requirement calls into question the protectiveness of the selected remedy”, 55 Fed. Reg.
8757 (March 8, 1990). The NC Classifications and Water Quality Standards Applicable to the
Groundwater of North Carolina, NCAC Title 15A Subchapter 2L, (NC 2L) on which several of the
remedial goals are based were last amended on November 1, 2025. Title 15A of the North Carolina
Administrative Code, Subchapter 2L (NCAC 2L) is a Chemical-Specific State ARAR for this Site.

25 feet, are sampled using Supersleeve no-purge sampling devices. These samplers, which "core" a 5-foot
interval of standing groundwater, are lowered by weighted tether to the screen-depth interval of each
monitoring well and coupled to the underside of the well 's locking, expandable-gasket well-riser cap. Per
sampler vendor recommendation, sample collection takes place a minimum of one week after sampler
emplacement, to allow equilibration of the groundwater column in the well. For this project, the samplers
are replaced after an annual/semi-annual event and left until the next sampling event, 6-12 months later.

         Monitoring wells constructed with above-grade risers and locking outer casings, and having static
groundwater depths less than 25 feet, will be sampled by peristaltic pump. Dedicated sample tubing is
installed to the screen depth of each well. The tubing is affixed securely through a perforated PVC riser
cap inside the well's locking outer casing, allowing NCDEQ personnel to conduct drive-up, no-purge
sampling after the initial one-week period of groundwater column equilibration. Residential wells (where
applicable are purged until water-quality indicator parameters (temperature, pH, specific conductivity, and
turbidity) have reached a stabilized state. Samples are collected from the tap closest to the wellhead and
prior to any holding tanks or filtration systems.

        Samples are labeled, packed in an iced cooler, and overnight shipped to a DEQ Superfund state
contract laboratory for VOC and SVOC analysis. Pace Analytical Services (formerly Shealey
Environmental Services, Inc.), in West Columbia, SC was the contract lab until 2024, when the contract
was awarded to Eurofins Environmental Testing in Cary, NC. Samples are analyzed for volatile organic
compounds (VOCs) and polynuclear aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) by USEPA methods 8260 and 8270
(or equivalent), respectively.

4.1 Scope of Work and Sampling Protocols

          Up to twenty-one groundwater monitoring wells are presently specified for MNA sampling.
However, MW-16 has a bent stickup and upper inner casing, likely due to collision by farm equipment, and
MW-29s typically either is dry or yields visible creosote product. Sample locations are illustrated in Figure
2-1 and summarized in Table 2. The Supersleeve no-purge sampling method consists of lowering one or
more Supersleeve polyethylene bags (on a weighted tether) into a monitoring well's screened interval.
Following a groundwater equilibration period, the unit is withdrawn from the well, filling the Supersleeve
and "coring" a standing groundwater volume from the screen interval. Supersleeve units compatible with
on-site monitoring wells measure 1.75-inch diameter and 36 inches, 48 inches, or 60 inches in length; when
filled, they produce sample volumes of 1.0 L to > 1.5 L, respectively.

        All groundwater samples were collected and handled in accordance with the USEPA Region 4
SESD Field Branches Quality System and Technical Procedures. Because the monitoring wells are
allowed to pre-equilibrate with minimal pre-sample purging, no groundwater field parameter measurements
(temperature, pH, conductivity, turbidity) were conducted. The Site Health and Safety Plan is included in
Appendix A while the field activity notes and sampling log are included in Appendix B.

quality control/quality assurance for this investigation consist of one duplicate sample, collected at

5.1 ROD Compounds

       Nine (9) of the nineteen monitoring wells sampled during NCDEQ' s February 2025 O&M
sampling event contained detectable concentrations of ROD VOCs; benzene was the only VOC
contaminant of concern (CoC) detected at concentration exceeding the Site RG. Benzene was not detected
in the MW-06i sample, in contrast with the previous 2022 event. An additional two (2) ROD VOC
compounds (Ethylbenzene, Toluene), and two non-ROD VOC compound (Isopropyl benzene and Styrene)
were detected in monitoring wells at levels below their respective Site RGs or NCAC 2L standard.

        Detectable concentrations of one to seven (7) NCAR PAH SVOC CoCs were found in the
groundwater samples collected from nine (9) of the nineteen sampled monitoring wells. Four of these
(Acenaphthene, Carbazole, 2-Methylnaphthalene, and Naphthalene) were shown to exceed the Site RGs.

the eight Carcinogenic PAH SVOC CoCs was detected during the February 2025 sampling event.

monitoring wells. Dibenzofuran concentrations exceeded the NCAC 2L standard in samples from MW-5s,
MW-07s, MW-23s, and MW-30s. Non-ROD SVOC compound 1, 1-Biphenyl, which currently has no
assigned NCAC 2L standard, was detected in samples from three monitoring wells.

6.0 Conclusions

        The on-site pump and treat system has been shut down since l\1arch 2019. MNA O&M activity
reverted to an annual basis following the November 29-30, 2021, sampling event. No sampling was
conducted in 2023 or 2024, due to the need to hire and train new personnel to replace recent retirees

        Nineteen groundwater monitoring wells were sampled during the Annual February 2025 O&M
phase of the Cape Fear Wood Preserving Site MNA remedy. The collected groundwater samples were
submitted to Eurofins Environmental Testing and analyzed for VOCs and SVOCs. One VOC (Benzene),
four NCAR PAH SVOCs (Acenaphthene, Carbazole, 2-Methylnaphthalene, and Naphthalene) were
detected in Site groundwater samples at levels exceeding the Site RGs. Anomalous detections of
Naphthalene at two site-peripheral monitoring wells (MW-18s and MW-19s) during the November 29,
2022 sampling event did not recur in 2025.

7.0 Observations

          Certain site monitoring wells, including MW-3s, MW-5s, MW-7s, MW-20s and MW-23s, have
exhibited considerable, long-term, cyclical or semi-cyclical fluctuation in SVOC concentrations, often
occurring at 2-to-4-year intervals. However, the analytical data from the February 26, 2025 sampling event
indicated significant decreases in CoC concentrations, compared to sampling events in 2021-2022, in
several monitoring wells including MW-3s, MW-5s, MW-6i, MW-7s, MW-22s, MW-25s, and MW-33s.
In particular, concentrations of several SVOCs in MW-3S , located across South Reilly Road from the
facility, went from maximum historical levels to non-detect between the 2022 and 2025 events. MW-5s
had SVOC detections in 2025, but showed a similar pattern to MW-3s. In contrast, far-western monitoring
well MW-23s, after cyclical fluctuations from detections to non-detections, experienced a sharp increase in
SVOC concentrations after 2021, reaching its highest ever recorded SVOV concentrations during the 2025
sampling event.

        The l\1arch 18 & 19, 2019 baseline sampling event indicated no impact of site related groundwater
conditions on the two (2) off-site residential wells (Hess and S&W Concrete). These two wells were not
sampled during the November 2022 or February 2025 sampling events but will be reevaluated should
conditions warrant.
