                                  I.      INTRODUCTION

        The Commodity Futures Trading Commission (“Commission”) has reason to believe that
from at least January 2021 to the present (“Relevant Period”), Banco Santander, S.A. (“BSSA”)
and Santander US Capital Markets LLC (“SanCap”) (collectively, “Respondents”) violated, as
set forth below, Sections 4g, 4s(f)(1)(B), 4s(g)(1), and 4s(h)(1)(B) of the Commodity Exchange
Act (“Act”), 7 U.S.C. §§ 6g, 6s(f)(1)(B), 6s(g)(1), 6s(h)(1)(B), and Commission Regulations
(“Regulations”) 1.35(a)(1)(i) and (iii), 23.201(a)(1), 23.202(a)(1) and (b)(1), 23.602(a), and
166.3, 17 C.F.R. §§ 1.35(a)(1)(i), (iii), 23.201(a)(1), 23.202(a)(1), (b)(1), 23.602(a), 166.3
(2024). Therefore, the Commission deems it appropriate and in the public interest that public
administrative proceedings be, and hereby are, instituted to determine whether Respondents
engaged in the violations set forth herein and to determine whether any order should be issued
imposing remedial sanctions.

        In anticipation of the institution of an administrative proceeding, Respondents have
submitted an Offer of Settlement (“Offer”), which the Commission has determined to accept.
Without admitting or denying any of the findings or conclusions herein, Respondents consent to
the entry of this Order Instituting Proceedings Pursuant to Section 6(c) and (d) of the Commodity
Exchange Act, Making Findings, and Imposing Remedial Sanctions (“Order”), and acknowledge
service of this Order. 1

                                                II.      FINDINGS

The Commission finds the following:

A.       Summary

        During the Relevant Period, Respondents’ employees communicated using unapproved
methods. These written communications were sent and received by Respondents’ employees
and included messages related to Respondents’ businesses as Commission registrants that were
required to be maintained under Commission-mandated recordkeeping requirements. These
written communications were not captured and preserved by Respondents. As a result,
Respondents violated, as set forth below, Sections 4g, 4s(f)(1)(B), and 4s(g)(1) of the Act, 7
U.S.C. §§ 6g, 6s(f)(1)(B), 6s(g)(1), and Regulations 1.35, 23.201(a)(1), and 23.202(a)(1) and
(b)(1), 17 C.F.R. §§ 1.35(a)(1)(i) and (iii), 23.201(a)(1), 23.202(a)(1), (b)(1) (2024). In addition,
the use of unauthorized communication methods violated Respondents’ policies and procedures,
which prohibited such communications. Respondents did not implement a diligent supervisory
system to ensure compliance with Commission recordkeeping requirements and Respondents’
own policies and procedures, and therefore, Respondents failed to diligently supervise matters
related to Respondents’ businesses as Commission registrants in violation of Section 4s(h)(1)(B)
of the Act, 7 U.S.C. § 6s(h)(1)(B), and Regulations 23.602(a) and 166.3, 17 C.F.R. §§ 23.602(a),
166.3 (2024), as set forth below.

        In accepting Respondents’ Offer, the Commission recognizes Respondents’ exemplary
cooperation. The Commission’s recognition is reflected in a reduced penalty, with Respondents
receiving the maximum cooperation mitigation credit toward the full monetary penalty
appropriate for these violations.

C.      Facts

        Although Respondents’ policies and procedures prohibited the use of unapproved
communication methods, during the Relevant Period, Respondents’ employees engaged in
written communications related to Respondents’ Commission-regulated businesses that were not
monitored, captured, or preserved. Further, although no customer or market harm is known to
have occurred as a result, Commission recordkeeping requirements were not met, and
Respondents failed to diligently supervise their employees to ensure compliance.

        1.       Respondents’ Recordkeeping Failures

        During the Relevant Period, Respondents’ policies and procedures prohibited employees
from using unapproved communication methods, such as personal text messages, to engage in
business-related communications. Additionally, Respondents provided annual training and
regularly issued compliance bulletins regarding their communications policies.

        Messages sent through Respondents’ approved communication methods were monitored,
subject to review, and when appropriate, archived. However, messages sent using unapproved
communication methods, including over personal text messages, were not monitored, subject to
review, or archived.

        A sampling analysis conducted by Respondents, which included the personal devices of
approximately five percent of its U.S.-based associated persons (“APs”) over a six-month period
in 2021, revealed that all of the sampled APs violated Respondents’ communications policies
and procedures by using personal text messages to communicate with coworkers and/or market
participants about business activities. The analysis further revealed that the communications of
some of these sampled APs involved Respondents’ Commission-regulated businesses.
Respondents acknowledge that these results are likely representative of the issue more broadly.

        2.       Exemplary Cooperation

        Respondents cooperated with the Division of Enforcement (“Division”) throughout its
investigation. Respondents voluntarily collected and reviewed messages retrieved from the
personal devices of a sampling of Respondents’ APs and made presentations to Division staff
regarding Respondents’ Commission-regulated businesses, their electronic communications
compliance program, and the results of the sampling review.

        As part of their cooperation efforts, Respondents have significantly completed
remediation. Respondents undertook an internal review to identify and assess the scope of
potential violative communications. Respondents represent that they have enhanced their
policies and procedures, increased training concerning the use of unapproved communication

                                   III.    LEGAL DISCUSSION

A.      BSSA Failed to Keep Required Records in Violation of Sections 4s(f)(1)(B) and
        4s(g)(1) of the Act and Regulations 23.201(a) and 23.202(a)(1) and (b)(1).

         Section 4s(f)(1)(B) of the Act obligates a swap dealer to keep “books and records of all
activities related to its business as a swap dealer . . . in such form and manner and for such period
as may be prescribed by the Commission by rule or regulation.” 7 U.S.C. § 6s(f)(1)(B); see also
Section 4s(g)(1) of the Act, 7 U.S.C. § 6s(g)(1) (requiring swap dealers to maintain daily trading
records). Part 23 of the Regulations implement these recordkeeping requirements. 17 C.F.R. pt.
23 (2024).

         Pursuant to Regulation 23.201(a)(1), a swap dealer is required to “keep full, complete,
and systematic records, together with all pertinent data and memoranda, of all its swaps
activities,” including “[r]ecords of each transaction, including all documents on which
transaction information is originally recorded.” 17 C.F.R. § 23.201(a)(1) (2024). Further,
Regulation 23.202(a)(1) and (b)(1) requires, among other things, that a swap dealer keep daily
trading records of all swaps and related cash and forward transactions it executes, including,
specifically, a record of all oral and written communications provided or received concerning
quotes, solicitations, bids, offers, instructions, trading, and prices that led to the execution of a
swap transaction or the conclusion of a related cash or forward transactions. 17 C.F.R. §
23.202(a)(1), (b)(1).

       During the Relevant Period, as a result of its employees’ use of unapproved
communication methods, BSSA violated Sections 4s(f)(1)(B) and 4s(g)(1) of the Act and
Regulations 23.201(a)(1) and 23.202(a)(1) and (b)(1).

B.      SanCap Failed to Keep Required Records in Violation of Section 4g of the Act and
        Regulation 1.35(a)(1)(i) and (iii).

        Section 4g of the Act requires FCMs and other registrants to create and keep books and
records pertaining to transactions and positions in commodities for future delivery and in any
significant price discovery contract in such form and manner and for such period as may be
required by the Commission. 7 U.S.C. § 6g (2024). Regulation 1.35(a)(1), 17 C.F.R. § 1.35
(a)(1) (2024), sets forth some of the books and records that are required to be kept and
maintained by FCMs. Specifically, an FCM must:

        (i) Keep full, complete, and systematic records . . . of all transactions relating to
        its business of dealing in commodity interests and related cash and forward
        transactions, which shall include all orders (filled, unfilled, or canceled), . . . and

       dealing in commodity interests and related cash or forward transactions . . . ;

       quotes, solicitations, bids, offers, instructions, trading, and prices that lead to the

       forward transactions . . . .

       During the Relevant Period, as a result of its employees’ use of unapproved
communication methods, SanCap violated Section 4g of the Act and Regulation 1.35(a)(1)(i) and
(iii).

       C.      Respondents Failed to Diligently Supervise.

       Regulation 166.3 states:

       Each Commission registrant, except an associated person who has no supervisory
       duties, must diligently supervise the handling by its partners, officers, employees
       and agents (or persons occupying a similar status or performing a similar
       function) of all commodity interest accounts carried, operated, advised or
       introduced by the registrant and all other activities of its partners, officers,
       employees and agents (or persons occupying a similar status or performing a
       similar function) relating to its business as a Commission registrant.

17 C.F.R. § 166.3 (2024).

        A violation of Regulation 166.3 is demonstrated by showing either that: (1) the
registrant’s supervisory system was generally inadequate; or (2) the registrant failed to perform
its supervisory duties diligently. In the Matter of Murlas Commodities, Inc., CFTC No. 85-29,
1995 WL 523563, at *9 (Sept. 1, 1995) (consent order); In re GNP Commodities, Inc., CFTC
No. 89-1, 1992 WL 201158, at *17-19 (Aug. 11, 1992) (consent order) (providing that, even if
an adequate supervisory system is in place, Regulation 166.3 can still be violated if the
supervisory system is not diligently administered); Sansom Refining Co. v. Drexel Burnham
Lambert, Inc., CFTC No. 82-R448, 1990 WL 282783, at *11 (Feb. 16, 1990) (consent order)
(noting that, under Regulation 166.3, registrants have a “duty to develop procedures for the
detection and deterrence of possible wrongdoing by [their] agents” (internal quotation omitted));
see also In re Rosenthal Collins Grp., LLC, CFTC No. 12-18, 2012 WL 1242406, at *6 (Apr. 12,
2012) (consent order) (finding that respondent failed to perform supervisory duties diligently by
not following its compliance procedures that were in place). A violation under Regulation 166.3
is an independent violation for which no underlying violation is necessary. See In the Matter of
Thomas Collins, CFTC No. 94-13, 1997 WL 761927, at *10 (Dec. 10, 1997) (consent order).
Evidence of violations that “should be detected by a diligent system of supervision, either
because of the nature of the violations or because the violations have occurred repeatedly” is
probative of a failure to diligently supervise. In the Matter of Paragon Futures Ass’n, CFTC No.
88-18, 1992 WL 74261, at *14 (April 1, 1992) (consent order) (“The focus of any proceeding to



                                                   5
determine whether Rule 166.3 has been violated will be on whether [a] review [has] occurred
and, if it did, whether it was ‘diligent.’”).

        Section 4s(h)(1)(B) of the Act, requires “diligent supervision of the business of the
registered swap dealer.” 7 U.S.C. § 6s(h)(1)(B). The Regulations require that each swap dealer
“establish and maintain a system to supervise, and shall diligently supervise, all activities
relating to its business performed by its partners, members, officers, employees, and agents (or
persons occupying a similar status or performing a similar function).” Regulation 23.602(a), 17
C.F.R. § 23.602(a) (2024). The operative language of Regulation 23.602(a) is similar to the
language of the Commission’s longstanding supervision regulation for futures and options,
Regulation 166.3, 17 C.F.R. § 166.3 (2024). In re Commerzbank AG, CFTC No. 1903, 2018 WL
5921385, at *10-11 (Nov. 8, 2018) (consent order) (noting textual similarities between
Regulation 23.602 and Regulation 166.3 and applying case law concerning Regulation 166.3).

        During the Relevant Period, SanCap failed to supervise its FCM activities diligently, and
BSSA failed to supervise its swap dealer business activities diligently. Respondents failed to
maintain adequate supervisory systems to ensure that their employees complied with
Commission recordkeeping requirements and Respondents’ policies and procedures that
prohibited business-related communications on unapproved communication methods.
Respondents’ failures to diligently supervise are demonstrated by their failure to keep
Commission-required records and to detect violations of their policies and procedures.
Accordingly, SanCap failed to supervise diligently its officers, employees, and agents in
violation of Regulation 166.3; and BSSA failed to supervise diligently its officers, employees,
and agents, in violation of Section 4s(h)(1)(B) of the Act and Regulation 23.602(a).

                            IV.     FINDINGS OF VIOLATIONS

        Based on the foregoing, the Commission finds that, during the Relevant Period, BSSA
violated Sections 4s(f)(1)(B), 4s(g)(1), and 4s(h)(1)(B) of the Act, 7 U.S.C. §§ 6s(f)(1)(B),
6s(g)(1), 6s(h)(1)(B), and Regulations 23.201(a)(1), 23.202(a)(1) and (b)(1), and 23.602(a), 17
C.F.R. §§ 23.201(a)(1), 23.202(a)(1), (b)(1), 23.602(a) (2024); and SanCap violated Section 4g
of the Act, 7 U.S.C. § 6g, and Regulations 1.35(a)(1)(i) and (iii) and 166.3, 17 C.F.R. §§
1.35(a)(1)(i),(iii), 166.3 (2024).
