                                           I.       INTRODUCTION

        The Commodity Futures Trading Commission (“Commission”) has reason to believe that
from at least May 2019 to August 2023 (“Relevant Period”), SMBC Capital Markets, Inc.
(“Respondent”) violated, as set forth below, Sections 4s(f)(1)(B), 4s(g)(1), and 4s(h)(1)(B) of the
Commodity Exchange Act (“Act”), 7 U.S.C. §§ 6s(f)(1)(B), 6s(g)(1), 6s(h)(1)(B), and
Commission Regulations (“Regulations”) 23.201(a)(1), 23.202(a)(1) and (b)(1), and 23.602(a),
17 C.F.R. §§ 23.201(a)(1), 23.202(a)(1), (b)(1), 23.602(a) (2024). Therefore, the Commission
deems it appropriate and in the public interest that public administrative proceedings be, and
hereby are, instituted to determine whether Respondent engaged in the violations set forth herein
and to determine whether any order should be issued imposing remedial sanctions.

        In anticipation of the institution of an administrative proceeding, Respondent has
submitted an Offer of Settlement (“Offer”), which the Commission has determined to accept.
Without admitting or denying any of the findings or conclusions here, Respondent consents to
the entry of this Order Instituting Proceedings Pursuant to Section 6(c) and (d) of the Commodity
Exchange Act, Making Findings, and Imposing Remedial Sanctions (“Order”), and
acknowledges service of this Order.1

                                      II.     FINDINGS

The Commission finds the following:

A.     Summary

        During the Relevant Period, Respondent’s employees communicated using unapproved
methods. These written communications were sent and received by Respondent’s employees
and included messages related to Respondent’s business as a Commission registrant that were
required to be maintained under Commission-mandated recordkeeping requirements. These
written communications were not captured and preserved by Respondent. As a result,
Respondent violated Sections 4s(f)(1)(B) and 4s(g)(1) of the Act, 7 U.S.C. §§ 6s(f)(1)(B),
6s(g)(1), and Regulations 23.201(a)(1) and 23.202(a)(1) and (b)(1), 17 C.F.R. §§ 23.201(a)(1),
23.202(a)(1), (b)(1) (2024). In addition, the use of unauthorized communication methods
violated Respondent’s policies and procedures, which prohibited such communications.
Respondent did not implement a diligent supervisory system to ensure compliance with
Commission recordkeeping requirements and Respondent’s own policies and procedures, and
therefore Respondent failed to diligently supervise matters related to Respondent’s business as a
Commission registrant in violation of Section 4s(h)(1)(B) of the Act, 7 U.S.C. § 6s(h)(1)(B), and
Regulation 23.602(a), 17 C.F.R. § 23.602(a) (2024).

        In accepting Respondent’s Offer, the Commission recognizes Respondent’s exemplary
cooperation. The Commission’s recognition is reflected in a reduced penalty, with Respondent
receiving the maximum cooperation mitigation credit toward the full monetary penalty
appropriate for these violations.

B.     Respondent

       SMBC Capital Markets, Inc. is a financial services company with its headquarters in
New York, New York. Respondent has been registered with the Commission as a swap dealer
since December 31, 2012.

C.     Facts

        Although Respondent’s policies and procedures prohibited the use of unapproved
communication methods, during the Relevant Period, Respondent’s employees engaged in
written communications related to Respondent’s Commission-regulated business that were not
monitored, captured, or preserved. Further, although no customer or market harm is known to
have occurred as a result, Commission recordkeeping requirements were not met, and
Respondent failed to diligently supervise its employees to ensure compliance.

       1.      Respondent’s Recordkeeping Failures

       During the Relevant Period, Respondent’s policies and procedures prohibited employees
from using unapproved communication methods, such as personal text messages and WhatsApp,
to engage in business-related communications. Additionally, Respondent provided training to its
employees and issued policy reminders regarding Respondent’s communications policies.



                                                2
        Messages sent through Respondent’s approved communications methods were
monitored, subject to review, and when appropriate, archived. However, messages sent using
unapproved communication methods, including over personal WhatsApp, email, and text
messages, were not monitored, subject to review, or archived.

        In connection with the Division of Enforcement’s (“Division’s”) investigation,
Respondent conducted a review of its employees’ use of unapproved communications. This
review resulted in a determination that Respondent’s employees had used unapproved
communication methods to engage in firm business. A sampling analysis conducted by
Respondent, which included the personal devices of approximately ten percent of its associated
persons (“APs”) over at least a one-year period, revealed that approximately seventy percent of
the sampled APs violated Respondent’s communications policies and procedures by using
personal text messages or other unapproved methods to communicate with brokers, coworkers,
and/or market participants. The analysis further revealed that the communications of some of
these sampled APs involved Respondent’s Commission-regulated business. Respondent
acknowledges that these results are likely representative of the issue more broadly.

       2.      Exemplary Cooperation

       Respondent cooperated with the Division throughout its investigation. Respondent
voluntarily collected and reviewed messages retrieved from the personal devices of a sampling
of Respondent’s APs and made presentations to Division staff regarding, among other things,
Respondent’s Commission-regulated business and its electronic communications compliance
program.

        As part of its cooperation efforts, Respondent has completed remediation. Respondent
represents that it has enhanced its policies and procedures, provided new training concerning the
use of unapproved communication methods, approved new communication platforms to address
employee needs while ensuring compliance, implemented technological improvements, and
enhanced its disciplinary framework to mitigate the risk of employees communicating by
unapproved methods. Respondent further represents it disciplined staff that it found to have
violated its policies regarding unapproved communication methods.

                                 III.    LEGAL DISCUSSION

A.     Respondent Failed to Keep Required Records in Violation of Sections 4s(f)(1)(B)
       and 4s(g)(1) of the Act and Regulations 23.201(a) and 23.202(a)(1) and (b)(1).

        Section 4s(f)(1)(B) of the Act obligates a swap dealer to keep “books and records of all
activities related to its business as a swap dealer . . . in such form and manner and for such period
as may be prescribed by the Commission by rule or regulation.” 7 U.S.C. § 6s(f)(1)(B); see also
Section 4s(g)(1) of the Act, 7 U.S.C. § 6s(g)(1) (requiring swap dealers to maintain daily trading
records). Part 23 of the Regulations implement these recordkeeping requirements. 17 C.F.R. pt.
23 (2024).

        Pursuant to Regulation 23.201(a)(1), a swap dealer is required to “keep full, complete,
and systematic records, together with all pertinent data and memoranda, of all its swaps
activities,” including “[r]ecords of each transaction, including all documents on which


                                                 3
transaction information is originally recorded.” 17 C.F.R. § 23.201(a)(1) (2024). Further,
Regulation 23.202(a)(1) and (b)(1) requires, among other things, that a swap dealer keep daily
trading records of all swaps and related cash and forward transactions it executes, including,
specifically, a record of all oral and written communications provided or received concerning
quotes, solicitations, bids, offers, instructions, trading, and prices that led to the execution of a
swap transaction or the conclusion of a related cash or forward transactions. 17 C.F.R. §
23.202(a)(1), (b)(1).

       During the Relevant Period, as a result of its employees’ use of unapproved
communication methods, Respondent violated Sections 4s(f)(1)(B) and 4s(g)(1) of the Act and
Regulations 23.201(a) and 23.202(a)(1) and (b)(1).

B.      Respondent Failed to Diligently Supervise in Violation of Section 4s(h)(1)(B) of the
        Act and Regulation 23.602(a).

        Section 4s(h)(1)(B) requires “diligent supervision of the business of the registered swap
dealer[.]” 7 U.S.C. § 6s(h)(1)(B). Implementing this requirement, Regulation 23.602(a) requires
that each swap dealer “shall establish and maintain a system to supervise, and shall diligently
supervise, all activities relating to its business performed by its partners, members, officers,
employees, and agents (or persons occupying a similar status or performing a similar function).”
17 C.F.R. § 23.602(a) (2024). The operative language of Regulation 23.602 is similar to the
language of the Commission’s longstanding supervision regulation for futures and options,
Regulation 166.3, 17 C.F.R. § 166.3 (2024).

        Under Regulation 23.602(a), a violation is demonstrated by showing either that: (1) the
registrant’s supervisory system was generally inadequate; or (2) the registrant failed to perform
its supervisory duties diligently. See In re JPMorgan Chase Bank, N.A., CFTC No. 22-07, 2021
WL 6098347, at *6 (Dec. 17, 2021) (consent order); see also In re Commerzbank AG, CFTC No.
19-03, 2018 WL 5921385, at *10-11 (Nov. 8, 2018) (consent order) (noting textual similarities
between Regulation 23.602 and Regulation 166.3, applying case law concerning Regulation
166.3). Either showing “alone is sufficient to establish a violation of the supervision
requirement.” Id., at *10 (interpreting Regulation 23.602 in light of Regulation 166.3
precedents). No evidence of an underlying violation is necessary. In re Collins, CFTC No. 94-
13, 1997 WL 761927, at *10 (Dec. 10, 1997) (consent order) (interpreting Regulation 166.3).
Evidence of violations that “‘should be detected by a diligent system of supervision, either
because of the nature of the violations or because the violations have occurred repeatedly,’ is
probative of a failure to supervise.” See In re Bank of Nova Scotia, CFTC No. 20-26, 2020 WL
4926053, at *10 (Aug. 19, 2020) (consent order) (quoting In re Société Générale Int’l Ltd.,
CFTC No. 19-38, 2009 WL 4915485, at *7 (Sept. 30, 2019) (consent order)).

       Respondent failed to supervise its swap dealer business activities diligently during the
Relevant Period. Respondent failed to maintain an adequate supervisory system to ensure that its
employees complied with Commission recordkeeping requirements and Respondent’s policies
and procedures that prohibited business-related communications on unapproved communication
methods. Respondent’s failure to diligently supervise is demonstrated by its failure to keep
Commission-required records and to detect violations of its policies and procedures.



                                                   4
Accordingly, Respondent failed to supervise diligently its officers, employees, and agents, in
violation of Section 4s(h)(1)(B) of the Act and Regulation 23.602(a).

                            IV.     FINDINGS OF VIOLATIONS

        Based on the foregoing, the Commission finds that, during the Relevant Period,
Respondent violated Sections 4s(f)(1)(B), 4s(g)(1), and 4s(h)(1)(B) of the Act, 7 U.S.C. §§
6s(f)(1)(B), 6s(g)(1), 6s(h)(1)(B), and Regulations 23.201(a)(1), 23.202(a)(1) and (b)(1), and
23.602(a), 17 C.F.R. §§ 23.201(a)(1), 23.202(a)(1), (b)(1), 23.602(a) (2024).
